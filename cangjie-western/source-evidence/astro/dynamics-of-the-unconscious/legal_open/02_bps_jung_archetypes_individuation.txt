L0001  Jung says of the collective unconscious that it represents: a boundless expanse
L0002  full of unprecedented uncertainty... It is the world of water, where all life
L0003  floats in suspension; where the soul of everything living begins.
L0004  Jung considered that among the most empirically apparent archetypes are the
L0005  shadow, the anima and animus, and the Self.
L0006  To become conscious of the shadow involves recognising dark aspects of the
L0007  personality as present and real. This act is the essential condition for any
L0008  kind of self-knowledge and it therefore, as a rule, meets with considerable
L0009  resistance. (CW Vol.9 Part 2, p.8)
L0010  The most resistant aspects are frequently unconsciously projected outwards onto
L0011  others, which leads to a distorted view of the world and the tendency to see
L0012  menace and ill-intention on the part of others.
L0013  Whoever delves into the unconscious in the process of personal development risks
L0014  a confrontation with the shadow, a confrontation that represents the first test
L0015  of courage on the inner path and frightens off most people.
L0016  Jung distinguishes the archetype of the Self from the ego, which is the
L0017  constructed, conscious self. The most important symbols of the Self are geometrical
L0018  structures containing elements of the circle, and quadratic figures such as the
L0019  cross.
L0020  True individuation 'does not shut out from the world but gathers the world to
L0021  oneself'. Individuation is thus both the realisation of one's true self-identity
L0022  and at the same time the realisation that this self-identity is a manifestation
L0023  of the whole. (CW Vol.8, para 432)
L0024  How does individuation take place? Firstly the existence of inner conflict and
L0025  of the shadow must be recognised. Once the shadow is recognised and come to terms
L0026  with (one cannot correct or transform the problems it causes unless one brings
L0027  them into consciousness and acknowledge their existence) it becomes possible to
L0028  die to the mistaken idea of who we are and be reborn as the real Self.
L0029  This concept of death and rebirth is a major feature of many of the world's
L0030  great myths and psychospiritual traditions. Within the Western tradition Jung
L0031  found it best symbolised by the alchemical process... sulphur and mercury, the Red
L0032  King and the White Queen, representing the conscious and the unconscious.
L0033  All the archetypes, and indeed all genuine symbols, are ambiguous, paradoxical,
L0034  full of half glimpsed meanings, and inexhaustible in that they cannot be fully
L0035  interpreted. The very fact of this ambiguity also demonstrates that all genuine
L0036  symbols are symbols of transformation.
