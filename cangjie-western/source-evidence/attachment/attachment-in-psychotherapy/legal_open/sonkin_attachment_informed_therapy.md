# Evidence: Attachment-Informed Psychotherapy synthesis (Sonkin, Wallin/Fonagy/Bowlby based)
# Source: http://www.daniel-sonkin.com/attachpsych/attachment_psychotherapy.htm  (author open clinical page)
# Used for: Wallin Attachment in Psychotherapy — Bowlby 5 therapeutic tasks, reflective function, preoccupied/dismissing, rupture-repair, autonoetic consciousness
# NOTE: This is a secondary clinical synthesis grounded in Wallin (2007), Bowlby (1988), Fonagy. Confidence medium.
# Line-numbered excerpt

1. Complementarity between therapist and patient attachment style may facilitate the therapeutic alliance (Tyrrell, Dozier, Teague, Fallot 1995).
2. Infants are not capable of regulating their own emotions and arousal and therefore require the assistance of their caregiver. How the infant ultimately learns to regulate emotions depends heavily on how the caregiver(s) regulate their own emotions. This dyadic regulation never entirely disappears.
3. The "attunement" of the caregiver is critical to secure attachment patterns (Stern 1985). Parents who are sensitive to verbal and non-verbal cues are able to hold the infant's mind in their mind — mentalizing ability / reflective function (Fonagy, Target, Gergely, Jurist 2002).
4. With insecure infants and children, their particular behavioural coping mechanisms (of avoidance, resistance or approach/avoidance) may become more sophisticated, but the net result (over-activating or under-activating) will essentially continue as the individual ages.
5. Reflective function (Fonagy & Target, scored from AAI transcript): assessing the speaker's ability to reflect on their own inner experience, and at the same time reflect on the mind of others. A person with high reflective function exhibits a non-defensive willingness to engage emotionally, to make meaning of feelings without becoming overwhelmed or shutting down.
6. Tasks of attachment-informed psychotherapy according to Bowlby (1988):
   (1) Create a safe place / secure base for client to explore thoughts, feelings and experiences regarding self and attachment figures;
   (2) Explore current relationships with attachment figures;
   (3) Explore the relationship with the psychotherapist as an attachment figure;
   (4) Explore the relationship between early childhood attachment experiences and current relationships;
   (5) Find new ways of regulating attachment anxiety (emotional regulation) when the attachment behavioural system is activated.
7. Autonoetic / autobiographical / extended consciousness: episodic memory with a sense of self at the time (e.g. "my father beat me and I felt so angry and I still feel that way today"), distinct from noetic knowing of facts. Mediated by prefrontal cortex and hippocampus.
8. Three possible patient responses to "Why do you get angry when your partner withdraws?": (1) "It just upsets me" (low); (2) "It's been going on for years, I overreact" (medium); (3) "My father left when I was young; when my partner withdraws I feel rejected but overreact because of that" (high autonoetic coherence).
9. Mikulincer, Gillath, Shaver (2002): perceived threats activate the attachment behavioural system; adults with insecure attachment respond by hyperactivating or deactivating the system depending on attachment style.
10. Preoccupied individuals have learned to become hypervigilant regarding attachment figures; they hyperactivate attachment distress to stay connected or get attention. They need to: learn how past experiences affect current relationships; look less to others for soothing and soothe their own anxiety; realize they have choices; discover underlying emotions; find more constructive coping.
11. Dismissing clients over-rely on independence and devalue attachment to deal with fear of closeness, leading to loneliness. Treating deactivators involves connecting with the body and noticing changes in organismic state (core consciousness).
12. Unresolved loss and trauma leads to extreme emotion dysregulation; individuals regulate attachment distress through approach AND avoidance. When strongest it produces breakdown in cognition and affect: uncontrollable rage and dissociation. These individuals need to resolve previous traumas and losses to break the disorganized processes.
13. Rupture and repair: use the natural separations and ruptures in therapy (session endings, vacations, illnesses) to help the client develop more adaptive ways of coping with intense vulnerability.
14. Case Vignette Robert: 34yo, preoccupied, post-separation; jealous, dependent, verbally abusive; asks "What do I think he should do to get his wife back"; rants 10 minutes then forgets question; "people are never there when you need them". Treatment: help him self-regulate rather than use proximity maintenance; use natural ruptures as emotionally competent stimuli; connect body and label experiences; develop extended consciousness (past-present-future) and response flexibility.
15. Case Vignette Alison: preoccupied, angry, uses psychobabble, takes conversational turns; weak mother / rejecting father history; indicator of unresolved loss. Treatment: regulate anxiety access other emotions; build empathy/social cognition; possible mindfulness to shift PFC activation.
16. Caution: therapists must balance attachment conceptualisations with continual risk assessment, especially in domestic violence contexts; do not pathologize normal grief.
17. Earned security: motivated, self-reflective client who can hold others' minds, balanced perspective on childhood, follows Bowlby's protocol to build secure-attachment capacities.
