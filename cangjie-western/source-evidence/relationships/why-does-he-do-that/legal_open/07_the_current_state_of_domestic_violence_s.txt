00014	The Current Sate of Domestic…
00015	In the first three posts of this series, I laid out a collection of concerns regarding the direction that domestic violence work has taken over roughly the past two decades. The issues I’m raising are ones that have been brought to me by abused women themselves and by program staff, many of whom are also survivors. (Let me say again that the program staff are rarely the problem; the vast majority of them are fighting like hell to do what they think is right, but are finding themselves up against obstacle after obstacle, as I wrote about in Parts 1-3.)
00016	I promised that I’d finish by proposing solutions. I am going to put my plan of action in three categories:  1) What individuals can do;  2) What groups can do (including established programs for abused women; and 3) What both individuals and groups should be trying. Today’s post is just going to cover the first of these – this series continues!
00017	WHAT INDIVIDUALS CAN DO
00018	Currently and formerly abused women are going to have to take the movement against domestic violence back. It has been removed from the hands of survivors and taken over by professionals. How can the movement be reclaimed?
00019	1)  ABUSED WOMEN, AND THEIR ALLIES, CREATE THEIR OWN SUPPORT SERVICES AND NETWORKS, DIRECTED BY THE SURVIVORS
00020	The battered women’s movement was started by survivors and their allies, not by professionals. You don’t need anyone’s authorization, or any degree or certification, to start helping other survivors. If you live where services don’t exist, or you don’t agree with how services in your area are being run, start your own group.
00021	Please don’t be intimidated by people who tell you that you aren’t qualified to do this, or that you should “leave it to the professionals.” That’s exactly what you should
00022	not
00023	do. For the first decade or more of the battered women’s movement, survivors were providing most of the services and doing an awesome job, much better than the programs are typically doing nowadays.
00024	The first place to look for ideas and guidance on creating your own support network for survivors is the Justice Jones Facebook page
00025	So You Want to be a Justice Advocate
00026	. This page was created specifically for survivors who want to start their own support services on abusive relationships. (Please read all the information there carefully. It is
00027	not
00028	a crisis response page for survivors; it exists to provide training and mentoring to survivors for the specific work of advocating for other survivors. The page is open to the public, so posts and responses are
00029	not confidential
00030	.)
00031	Here are a couple of additional resources you can draw upon, though these are not specific to issues of domestic abuse:
00032	Creating and Facilitating Peer Support Groups
00033	How to Run a Peak Living Network Support Group
00034	If you’ve ever participated in a support group yourself, you’ve learned some valuable approaches to being a group leader. You can do it. Groups can meet in person or online, and you can structure them in whatever way you choose. For example, it’s up to you:
00035	*  how often the group meets
00036	*  who facilitates each meeting
00037	*  what requirements you’ll have for who is allowed to participate
00038	*  what rules or guidelines you will have for behavior during the group
00039	*  whether the meetings will follow have scheduled topics or not
00040	*  whether you’ll follow a particular book or written program for abused women
00041	It’s always valuable to get ideas and input from other women (or male allies), but what matters most is to run things the way you would like to see them run. That’s what will give you the most energy to keep the group going, and then to build a wider support network around the group,
00042	2)  ABUSED WOMEN, AND THEIR ALLIES, CREATE THEIR OWN ACTIVIST GROUPS, DIRECTED BY THE SURVIVORS
00043	Demanding social change is as important as providing services; in fact, the two efforts should really be working side by side and supporting each other. But as domestic violence work has increasingly been taken over by larger and larger non-profits, the aspect of the work that is about activism has vanished.
00044	The sad result of this split is that, although we now have more services available than ever (in most locations), the overall circumstances of abused women are considerably
00045	worse
00046	than they were fifteen years ago. The vastly increased corruption in the custody courts; the racist, class-prejudiced, and mother-blaming orientation typical of most child protective services; the huge cuts in public welfare benefits and public health resources (such as WIC and food stamps); and the general backward trends in policing and prosecution, moving back toward old habits of ignoring domestic violence and blaming the victims; all of these factors taken together make a crisis that no amount of service-provision can address.
00047	So we have to get back in the streets, abused women and their allies together. And, as Judith Herman discussed in her classis book
00048	Trauma and Recovery
00049	,
00050	survivors who get involved in fighting for justice tend to see better progress
00051	in their personal healing
00052	.
00053	Consider working with women in your geographical region, fighting for an end to male violence against women as
00054	a
00055	human rights issue
00056	(not as an issue of
00057	dysfunction
00058	, which is a misleading way to frame the problem, given its deep historical and societal roots). Work for social change by:
00059	1)  Holding marches and rallies that demand systemic change in the response to abused women, including calling out CPS, family courts, police, and prosecutors on their actions
00060	2)  Creating creative projects such as photo exhibits, framed collections of short writings by abused women about their experiences, clothesline projects, short plays, and other artistic ways to get the message across
00061	3)  Organizing and participating in non-violent civil disobedience actions, where some participants may choose to risk arrest, such as sit-ins, blockades, die-ins, and similar direct actions. Consider a range of other kinds of actions as well, including petitions, boycotts, public shaming of officials (such as family court judges), and picketing against human rights abusers at their workplaces or homes
00062	(For one example, mothers in New Jersey put on a march where they all wore t-shirts that had the name and photo of the most egregiously mother-abusing judge printed on them).
00063	4)  Build a stable, lasting women’s rights organization that becomes known and visible in your community, with a focus on combating male violence and tyranny against females (using all three of the above tactics, and more)
00064	Building solid and effective activist groups is hard work; I won’t pretend that it isn’t. For trauma survivors, organizing can sometimes be extra difficult, because of various triggers that group members are dealing with. But there are resources available to help you with your organizing, such as the Family Violence Response Initiative and the “So You Want to Be a Justice Advocate” FB page that I linked to above.
00065	You might also draw upon the book
00066	Strategy and Soul
00067	by Daniel Hudson
00068	, which tells the story of a specific social justice effort but mixes it in with lots of suggestions about how to make change happen in your community.
00069	And after I’m finished writing the remaining parts of this series, I’ll add a post that’s about basic principles in organizing for social change that I hope you’ll find helpful.

00085	Quick PSA for anyone trying to get a restraining order in California and Your Abuser is Evading Service or You Don’t Know His Address: Starting in January 2020, California now let’s you apply for “Alternate Service” if your abuser is evading service of restraining order documents and you keep having to continue the court hearing because of it. Your local court or victim services office may not know about this new rule (mine didn’t). The forms are called DV117 and DV205Info. Google those form names and the pdfs will come up.
00086	An attorney friend of mine alerted me to these new forms after mt abuser evaded sheriff service of restraining order hearing notices.  Since he wouldn’t come to the door and hung up on the sheriff when they called, I had to keep going back to court 6 times with a new hearing date!  The Victim Services office said I “just have to keep trying to find him”.  They were happy to continue my case indefinitely.  Luckily my friend sent me these forms.  I gave them to the judge and she is allowing him to be considered served by the court posting the hearing notices on their bulletin board.  Another option was the newspaper.  Both were free for me.
00087	This is a general comment on the DV Victim Services Unit in my county- YIKES!  They are rude, passive aggressive, and condescending to their clients.  DV victims are forced by the court to go through this unit.  The judge won’t even hear your case or accept paperwork unless you work directly with the Victim Services.  They’re all women but they treat us victims horribly, as if we’re the offenders.  Last time I was nearly in tears because I wasn’t prepared for the attack from the new advocate.  They seem to be getting worse and worse.  The more I try and stand up for myself the more they try and sabotage my efforts to get protection from my abuser (violence, stalking, threatening to murder me).  It’s really like the twilight zone.

00090	So, how do we fight for the innocent children witnessing and/or suffering domestic violence without DHS assuming the best thing is to remove the children from the home? I grew up in such a home where Mom suffered emotional and physical abuse and I witnessed it along with enduring emotional abuse. Thankfully, I had various adults throughout my childhood who gave me attention and believed in me, so I came through better off than most. Then, the flashbacks began after I had two children displaying sibling rivalry. Long story short, I began a thirty year journey of counseling, medication, and nervous breakdowns as I was still trying to get Mom to leave Dad. She finally did at age 87! Needless to say, I am VERY concerned about children who don’t have any adults in their lives to give them love and attention. It’s especially concerning during this COVID isolating time. I’m trying to find a way to reach these children or identify them without the wrong authorities stepping in. I recently completed my memoir and its purpose is to make people aware, give hope, and show how important it is to support children who are hurting or displaying troubling signs of stress.
