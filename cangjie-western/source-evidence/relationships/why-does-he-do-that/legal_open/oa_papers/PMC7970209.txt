00001	Johnson argued that coercive control is crucial in explaining heterogeneity in
00002	intimate partner violence, with such violence being more frequent, less
00003	reciprocal, and more often male-to-female aggression when it serves to exercise
00004	control over the partner. We assessed 280 Dutch forensic outpatients who had
00005	recently engaged in intimate partner violence on nonaggressive coercive control.
00006	Control showed significant, small to moderate, associations with more frequent
00007	past year acts of psychological aggression, physical assault, and sexual
00008	coercion and more frequently resulted in partner injury. Control was unrelated
00009	to reciprocity of partner violence. High controlling violence was enacted
00010	mostly, but not exclusively by men. Overall, while perhaps not having a uniquely
00011	strong association, our findings provide partial support for the role of
00012	coercive control in intimate partner violence and suggest it may benefit
00013	intimate partner violence risk assessment.
00014	Introduction
00015	Research shows great variation in findings regarding the frequency, reciprocity, and
00016	gender distribution of intimate partner violence (IPV). Johnson (1995; for expansion to dyads, see
00017	Johnson, 2008)
00018	reasoned that there may be two types of IPV: common couple violence and patriarchal
00019	terrorism. Common couple violence would be typically paired with less frequent
00020	assaults, be more reciprocal, and more equally often committed by men and women.
00021	Patriarchal terrorism would be typically paired with more frequent assaults, be more
00022	often unidirectional, and most often be man-to-female aggression. The crucial
00023	element distinguishing the two types of IPV, however, is not the frequency,
00024	reciprocity, or gender distribution of the violence but rather the psychological
00025	dynamic leading to the violence, according to Johnson (1995): Common couple violence is
00026	reasoned to result from conflict between the couple, whereas the patriarchal
00027	terrorism type of violence reflects a means to exert control over the partner.
00028	Coercive control can be exerted in several ways (e.g., economically, socially,
00029	sexually), with the exertion of violence being just one of the means to achieve
00030	control over the partner.
00031	Several studies have found support for Johnson’s (1995) predictions on the role of
00032	control in IPV (see, e.g., Graham-Kevan & Archer, 2003; Hardesty et al., 2015; Leone, Johnson, Cohan, & Lloyd, 2004;
00033	for a review, see Johnson,
00034	2008). Johnson and
00035	Leone (2005), for instance, found that victimization by patriarchal
00036	terrorism was more severe and more persistent, than IPV in the context of common
00037	couple violence. These studies are, however, not without limitations. First, the
00038	theory is tested mostly by its developer, calling for independent replication.
00039	Indeed, as recognized by Johnson
00040	(2008), “I could cite very few studies that were specifically designed to
00041	answer even the most basic questions about the differences among intimate terrorism.
00042	. .and situational couple violence” (p. 85). Second, the dichotomization between the
00043	two types of violence often rests on an arbitrary cutoff point (in Johnson and Leone, 2005:
00044	0-2 vs. 3-7 controlling behaviors). As control is a dimension rather than a
00045	dichotomized construct, analyses using control as a continuum are not only more
00046	powerful but also more appropriate. Third, the measurement of coercive control was
00047	often construed ad hoc based on the (archival) data. Few studies used measures that
00048	were a priori designed to assess coercive control. Fourth, a crucial test for the
00049	theory is that the key variable—controlling behaviors—consists of nonviolent forms
00050	of control. If not, the distinction made by Johnson (1995) would be primarily one
00051	between less versus more severe forms of violence. Among the items assessing
00052	control, however, were often also items that describe emotional forms of IPV (e.g.,
00053	“calls you names or puts you down in front of others”). In sum, more work is needed
00054	to test the idea that the crucial variable in understanding heterogeneity in IPV is
00055	nonviolent coercive control.
00056	Understanding the role of control in IPV may not only help to explain variation in
00057	research findings regarding IPV frequency, reciprocity, and gender distribution, but
00058	also be of clinical relevance for IPV risk assessment and risk management. The
00059	current study tests the role of nonviolent coercive control in IPV. We assessed IPV
00060	perpetrators on nonviolent controlling behaviors. From Johnson (1995), it was predicted that
00061	attempts to control one’s partner would be associated with more frequent IPV, less
00062	reciprocal IPV, and more often committed by men.
00063	Method
00064	The current study made use of the initial (T0) data of a larger study (Buitelaar, Posthumus, Scholing,
00065	& Buitelaar, 2014). Briefly, participants were recruited in two
00066	locations of a Dutch center for outpatient forensic mental health care, where they
00067	had been referred to by court, the probation service, or the primary health care
00068	service because of having committed IPV.
00069	Participants
00070	Patients were included if they were at least 18 years old, spoke sufficient
00071	Dutch, provided written informed consent, and had engaged into IPV in the last 8
00072	weeks prior to intake. The measure of controlling behaviors was added in October
00073	2013 and the study was closed in 2017. We included the data of all patients who
00074	completed measures of coercive control and IPV (n=285),
00075	excluding data from five patients with >10% missing data on the measures of
00076	coercive control (Lilienfeld
00077	& Widows, 2005). The final sample (n = 280)
00078	consists of 224 males (80%) and 56 females (20%) and is described in Table 1. It is worth
00079	mentioning that, in the past year, 64% of the patients had engaged into severe
00080	psychological aggression, 37% into severe physical assault, 17% had inflicted
00081	severe injury to their partner, and 4% had engaged into severe sexual
00082	coercion.

00099	Procedure
00100	After checking inclusion criteria and providing written informed consent, the
00101	patient was given a paper and pencil booklet consisting of a series of
00102	questionnaires including the Intimate Partner Violence Control Scale (IPVCS),
00103	Conflict Tactics Scale (CTS)-2, and the PDQ-R. There was an opportunity to ask
00104	questions and clarify survey items. Patients individually completed the booklet
00105	at their own pace in their preferred location and either mailed or brought it
00106	back.
00107	Measures
00108	Control: IPVCS
00109	The IPVCS (Bledsoe &
00110	Sar, 2011) consists of 16 items (e.g., “I wish I had more say
00111	over the kinds of things my partner does with his or her friends”) that
00112	assess nonaggressive controlling behaviors on a scale from 1
00113	(never) to 5 (very often). Items were
00114	summed to provide a total score for controlling behaviors. Cronbach’s alpha
00115	of the IPVCS was α = .82.
00116	IPV Frequency: CTS-2
00117	We used the 34 items of the CTS (Straus, Hamby, & Warren, 2003)
00118	that assess physical assault (e.g., “I threw something at my partner that
00119	could hurt” [minor], “I used a knife or gun on my partner” [severe]), injury
00120	(e.g., “My partner felt physical pain the next day because of a fight we
00121	had” [minor], “My partner had a broken bone from a fight with me” [severe]),
00122	psychological aggression (e.g., “I insulted or swore at my partner” [minor],
00123	“I threatened to hit or throw something at my partner” [severe]), and sexual
00124	coercion (e.g., “I made my partner have sex without a condom” [minor], “I
00125	used threats to make my partner have sex” [severe]) toward the partner in
00126	the last 8 weeks. The response options were never, 1 time, 2 times, 3 to 5
00127	times, 6 to 10 times, 11 to 20 times, or more than 20 times. Following the
00128	CTS-2 manual, we calculated severity scores for IPV in the last year, with
00129	levels 0 = no (neither minor nor severe items endorsed), 1 = minor (at least
00130	one minor, but no severe items endorsed), 2 = severe (at least one severe
00131	item endorsed).
00132	IPV Reciprocity: CTS-2
00133	The patient assessed not only acts for perpetration but also for
00134	victimization by their partner. Following Graham-Kevan and Archer (2003), we
00135	subtracted the frequency of physical assault, injury, psychological
00136	aggression, and sexual coercion committed by the partner from the frequency
00137	of physical assault, injury, psychological aggression, and sexual coercion
00138	committed by the patient to obtain a measure of reciprocity of physical
00139	assault, injury, psychological aggression, and sexual coercion. Scores vary
00140	from −2 to +2. A positive score means that the patient engages more often
00141	into violence than the partner, a score around zero indicates that the
00142	violence is reciprocal, and a negative score suggests that the patient
00143	engages less often in violence than the partner.
00144	Statistical Analyses
00145	Because control is a dimension rather than a dichotomized construct, statistical
00146	analyses using control as a continuum are most appropriate. Because of the
00147	ordinal nature of the IPV severity scores, we tested predictions regarding IPV
00148	frequency and IPV reciprocity by calculating Spearman rho correlations between
00149	coercive control and IPV frequency and reciprocity. Because missing items were
00150	very rare (≤2.5% per measure), we used pairwise deletions. Following Cohen (1988),
00151	correlations of .1, .3, and .5 can be regarded as small, moderate, and large
00152	effects, respectively.
00153	Although Johnson (1995,
00154	2008) considers
00155	control to be a continuum, dimensional analyses do not allow to test the
00156	prediction that men are more likely than women to engage into controlling
00157	violence (i.e., in this prediction sex and control are phrased as categorical
00158	variables). The sex asymmetry prediction requires identification of the subgroup
00159	of patients engaging in high controlling violence. Because all patients included
00160	in this study had recently engaged into IPV, we relied solely on our measure of
00161	coercive control for this categorization. Specifically, we ran a hierarchical
00162	cluster analysis using Ward’s method with squared Euclidean distance as the
00163	proximity measure on the IPVCS items, determining the optimum number of clusters
00164	based on subjective assessment of the change in agglomeration coefficients. Once
00165	we have identified clusters of low versus high controlling patients, we assessed
00166	the proportion of males in the high controlling cluster.

00170	engaging into sexual coercion (see the first line of Table 2). Coercive control was essentially
00171	unrelated to reciprocity of physical assault, injury, psychological aggression, and
00172	sexual coercion (see also first line of Table 2).

00211	Discussion
00212	Our study aimed to test key predictions from Johnson’s (1995) typology of IPV that
00213	assigns a central role to coercive control in explaining IPV heterogeneity. We found
00214	partial support for the evaluated predictions: Coercive control was indeed related
00215	to more frequent IPV, and mostly enacted by men, but we found no support for the
00216	prediction that control would be associated with unidirectional violence.
00217	Coercive control was positively associated with IPV frequency, with small to moderate
00218	effect sizes (.23 ≤ r ≤ .36). To assess the importance of coercive
00219	control, it may be worthwhile to compare the associative strength of coercive
00220	control with that of other risk factors for IPV. The mean effect size of 26 risk
00221	factors in the meta-analysis of Stith Smith, Penn, Ward, and Tritt (2004) was r = .22.
00222	Thus, while perhaps not having a uniquely strong association, our study provides
00223	further evidence that coercive control is associated with IPV, suggesting it may be
00224	worthwhile for considering coercive control in risk assessment.
00225	The majority of our patients engaging into controlling violence were men,
00226	substantiating the idea that controlling violence is more often committed by men
00227	than by women. Note that this finding is qualified by the fact that our entire
00228	sample was predominantly male. Furthermore, while our findings confirm a sex
00229	asymmetry, we found no support for the idea that controlling violence would be
00230	committed “almost exclusively” (Johnson, 1995, p. 286) or “almost entirely” (Johnson, 2008, p. 2) by men, with 29% of
00231	the controlling violence perpetrated by women.
00232	Control was unrelated to reciprocity of IPV in this sample. Irrespective of control,
00233	the vast majority of patients had a reciprocity score of 0 ± 1 (indicative of
00234	reciprocal violence) for psychological aggression (97%), physical assault (89%),
00235	injury (94%), and sexual coercion (97%).
00236	This study is not without limitations. First, as in many studies on IPV, we relied
00237	upon self-report. Perpetrators of IPV may, however, lack insight into their own
00238	behavior and/or underreport aggression and psychopathology. Second, our findings are
00239	constrained to a predominantly male forensic outpatient sample and cannot be assumed
00240	to speak to the nature of IPV in the entire population (a logical error known as the
00241	clinical fallacy).
00242	Our study also had important strengths, including a fairly substantial forensic
00243	sample, a dedicated measure of nonaggressive coercive control, and analyses
00244	considering coercive control across the whole continuum. An additional strength may
00245	be the study setting. Previous studies on IPV have often used samples that either
00246	greatly reduced the chance of observing controlling violence (e.g., undergraduates)
00247	or of noncontrolling violence (e.g., inmates). In contrast, our sample may be
00248	regarded as “mixed” in the sense of including most likely both controlling violence
00249	(a forensic sample; high frequency of IPV) and noncontrolling violence (treatment
00250	was nonmandatory for the majority of patients). As such, our study provided an
00251	important test of the role of control in IPV.
00252	Forensic treatment is more effective when based on the risk–need–responsivity (RNR)
00253	model (Andrews & Bonta,
00254	2010). In accordance with the RNR principles, treatment intensity is
00255	adjusted to the risk of recidivism (risk principle), the treatment goals are related
00256	to the dynamic risk factors (need principle), and the treatment is adapted to the
00257	capacity of the client (responsivity principle). Based on the results of this study
00258	that control is associated with more frequent past year acts of psychological
00259	aggression, physical assault, and sexual coercion and more frequently resulted in
00260	partner injury, assessing coercive control could become part of risk assessment with
00261	more intensive treatment recommended for controlling IPV.
