00013	Early in my career I worked with David,* a handsome, intelligent 
00014	client who was a professional actor. One day David came to see 
00015	me after an unsuccessful audition. Beside himself, he burst out: "I 
00016	never let on to anyone, but I know that I'm really very ugly; it's 
00017	so stupid that I'm trying to be an actor when I'm so painful to look 
00018	at." 
00019	David's childhood was characterized by emotional abuse, neglect 
00020	and abandonment. The last and unwanted child of a large family, 
00021	his alcoholic father repeatedly terrorized him. To make matters 
00022	worse, his family frequently humiliated him by reacting to him 
00023	with exaggerated looks of disgust. His older brother's favorite 
00024	gibe, accompanied by a nauseated grimace, was, "I can't stand 
00025	looking at you. The sight of you makes me sick!" David was so 
00026	traumatized by the contempt with which his family had treated him that he was easily 
00027	triggered by anything but the most benign expression on my face. If he came into 
00028	session already triggered, he would often project disgust onto me, no matter how much 
00029	genuine goodwill and regard I felt for him at the time.  
00030	David was so traumatized by the contempt with which his family had treated 
00031	him that he was easily triggered by anything but the most benign expression 
00032	on my face. 
00033	I have come to call these reactions, typical of David and of many other clients over the 
00034	years, emotional flashbacks—sudden and often prolonged regressions ("amygdala 
00035	hijackings") to the frightening and abandoned feeling-states of childhood. They are 
00036	accompanied by inappropriate and intense arousal of the fight/flight instinct and the 
00037	sympathetic nervous system. Typically, they manifest as intense and confusing 
00038	episodes of fear, toxic shame, and/or despair, which often beget angry reactions 
00039	against the self or others. When fear is the dominant emotion in an emotional 
00040	flashback, the individual feels overwhelmed, panicky or even suicidal. When despair 
00041	predominates, it creates a sense of profound numbness, paralysis and an urgent need 
00042	to hide. Feeling small, young, fragile, powerless and helpless is also common in 
00043	emotional flashbacks. Such experiences are typically overlaid with toxic shame, which, 
00044	as described in John Bradshaw's Healing The Shame That Binds, obliterates an 
00045	individual's self-esteem with an overpowering sense that she is as worthless, stupid, 
00046	contemptible or fatally flawed, as she was viewed by her original caregivers. Toxic 
00047	shame inhibits the individual from seeking comfort and support, and in a reenactment 
00048	of the childhood abandonment she is flashing back to, isolates her in an overwhelming 
00049	and humiliating sense of defectiveness. Clients who view themselves as worthless, 
00050	defective, ugly or despicable are showing signs of being lost in an emotional flashback. 
00051	When stuck in this state, they often polarize affectively into intense self-hate and self-
00052	disgust, and cognitively into extreme and virulent self-criticism.

00080	Numerous clients tell me that the concept of an emotional flashback brings them a 
00081	great sense of relief. They report that for the first time they are able to make some 
00082	sense of their extremely troubled lives. Some get that their addictions are misguided 
00083	attempts to self-medicate. Some understand the inefficacy of the myriad psychological 
00084	and spiritual answers they pursued, and are in turn feel liberated from a shaming 
00085	plethora of misdiagnoses. Some can now frame their extreme episodes of risk taking 
00086	and self-destructiveness as desperate attempts to distract 
00087	themselves from their pain. Many experience hope that they 
00088	can rid themselves of the habit of amassing evidence of 
00089	defectiveness or craziness. Many report a budding recognition 
00090	that they can challenge the self-hate and self-disgust that 
00091	typically thwarts their progress in therapy.  
00092	Emotional Neglect: A Primary Cause of Complex PTSD? 
00093	 
00094	Early on in working with this model, I was surprised that a 
00095	number of clients with moderate and sometimes minimal 
00096	sexual or physical childhood abuse were plagued by emotional 
00097	flashbacks. Over time, however, I realized that these 
00098	individuals had suffered extreme emotional neglect: the kind of neglect where no 
00099	caretaker was ever available for support, comfort or protection. No one liked them, 
00100	welcomed them, or listened to them. No one had empathy for them, showed them 
00101	warmth, or invited closeness. No one cared about what they thought, felt, did, wanted, 
00102	or dreamed of. Such trauma victims learned early in life that no matter how hurt, 
00103	alienated, or terrified they were, turning to a parent would actually exacerbate their 
00104	experience of rejection.  
00105	The child who is abandoned in this way experiences the world as a terrifying place. I 
00106	think about how humans were hunter-gatherers for most of our time on this planet—
00107	the child's survival and safety from predators during the first six years of life during 
00108	these times depended on being in very close proximity to an adult. Children are wired 
00109	to feel scared when left alone, and to cry and protest to alert their caretakers when 
00110	they are. But when the caretakers turn their backs on such cries for help, the child is 
00111	left to cope with a nightmarish inner world—the stuff of which emotional flashbacks are 
00112	made.  
00113	Because of this, emotional flashbacks can best be understood as the key symptom of 
00114	Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder, a syndrome afflicting many adults who 
00115	experienced ongoing abuse or neglect in childhood. As described by leading trauma 
00116	theorist Judith Herman (Trauma and Recovery) and renowned PTSD researcher Bessel 
00117	van der Kolk, Complex PTSD is caused by "prolonged, repeated trauma" and "a history 
00118	of subjection to totalitarian control" such as happens in extremely dysfunctional 
00119	families. It is distinguished from the more familiar type of PTSD in which the trauma is 
00120	specific and defined; because of the prolonged nature of the trauma, Complex PTSD 
00121	can be even more virulent and pervasively damaging in its effects. (Complex PTSD has 
00122	not yet been included in the DSM.)  
00123	Emotional flashbacks can best be understood as the key symptom of Complex 
00124	Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder, a syndrome afflicting many adults who 
00125	experienced ongoing abuse or neglect in childhood. 
00126	Ongoing experience convinces me that some children respond to pervasive emotional 
00127	neglect and abandonment by over-identifying or even merging their identity with the 
00128	inner critic and adopting an intense form of perfectionism that triggers them into 
00129	painful abandonment flashbacks every time they are less than perfect or perfectly 
00130	
00131	pleasing. When I encourage such clients to free-associate 
00132	during their emotional flashbacks, I frequently hear a version 
00133	of this toxic shame spiral: "If only I were perfect. If only I were 
00134	an ‘A' student…a baseball hero…a beauty queen…a saint. If 
00135	only I weren't so stupid and selfish, then maybe they'd love 
00136	me. But who am I kidding? I'll never be anywhere near that, 
00137	because I'm just a piece of shit. Who in the world could ever 
00138	care about someone so pathetic?" 
00139	Responding Functionally to Emotional Flashbacks 
00140	Emotional flashbacks strand clients in the cognitions and 
00141	feelings of danger, helplessness and hopelessness that 
00142	characterized their original abandonment, when there was no 
00143	safe parental figure to go to for comfort and support. Hence, Complex PTSD is now 
00144	accurately being identified by some traumatologists as an attachment disorder. 
00145	Emotional flashback management, therefore, needs to be taught in the context of a 
00146	safe relationship. Clients need to feel safe enough with the therapist to describe their 
00147	humiliation and overwhelm, and the therapist needs to feel comfortable enough to 
00148	provide the empathy and calm support that was missing in the client's early 
00149	experience.  
00150	Because most emotional flashbacks do not have a visual or memory component to 
00151	them, the triggered individual rarely realizes that she is re-experiencing a traumatic 
00152	time from childhood. Psychoeducation is therefore a fundamental first step in the 
00153	process of helping clients understand and manage their flashbacks. Most of my clients 
00154	experience noticeable relief when I explain Complex PTSD to them. The diagnosis 
00155	resonates deeply with their intuitive understanding of their suffering. When they 
00156	recognize that their sense of overwhelm initially arose as a normal instinctual response 
00157	to their traumatic circumstances, they begin to shed the belief that they are crazy, 
00158	hopelessly oversensitive, and/or incurably defective.  
00159	 
00160	Without help in the midst of an emotional flashback, clients typically find no recourse 
00161	but their own particular array of primitive, self-injuring defenses to their unmanageable 
00162	feelings. These dysfunctional responses generally manifest in four ways: [1] fighting or 
00163	over-asserting oneself in narcissistic ways such as misusing power or promoting 
00164	excessive self-interest; [2] fleeing obsessive-compulsively into activities such as work 
00165	addiction, sex and love addiction, or substance abuse ("uppers"); [3] freezing in 
00166	numbing, dissociative ways such as sleeping excessively, over-fantasizing, or tuning 
00167	out with TV or medications ("downers"); [4] fawning codependently in self-abandoning 
00168	ways such as putting up with narcissistic bosses or abusive partners.  
00169	I find that most clients can be guided to see the harmfulness of their previously 
00170	necessary, but now outmoded, defenses as a misfiring of their fight, flight, freeze, or 
00171	fawn responses. In the context of a secure therapeutic alliance, they can begin to 
00172	replace these defenses with healthy, stress-ameliorating responses. I introduce this 
00173	phase of the work by giving the client the list of 13 cognitive, affective, somatic and 
00174	behavioral techniques (listed at the end of this article) to utilize outside of the session. 
00175	I elaborate on these techniques in our sessions as well.  
00176	As clients begin to respond more functionally to being triggered, opportunities arise 
00177	more frequently for working with flashbacks in session. In fact, it often seems that their 
00178	unconscious desire for mastery "schedules" their flashbacks to occur just prior to or 
00179	during sessions. I recently experienced this with a client who rushed into my office five 
00180	minutes late, visibly flushed and anxious. She opened the session by exclaiming, "I'm 
00181	
00182	such a loser. I can't do anything right. You must be sick of working with me." This was 
00183	someone who had, on previous occasions, accepted and even been moved by my 
00184	validation of her ongoing accomplishments in our work. Based on what she had 
00185	uncovered about her mother's punitive perfectionism in previous sessions, I was certain 
00186	that her being late had triggered an emotional flashback. In this moment, she was 
00187	most likely experiencing what Susan Vaughan's MRI research (The Talking Cure) 
00188	describes as a gross over-firing of right-brain emotional processing with a decrease in 
00189	cognitive processing in the left brain. Vaughan interprets this as a temporary loss of 
00190	access to left-brain knowledge and understanding. This appears to be a mechanism of 
00191	dissociation, and in this instance, it rendered my client amnesiac of my high regard for 
00192	our work together.  
00193	I believe this type of dissociation also accounts for the recurring disappearance of 
00194	previously established trust that commonly occurs with emotional flashbacks. This 
00195	phenomenon makes it imperative that we psychoeducate clients that flashbacks can 
00196	cause them to forget that proven allies are in fact still reliable, and that they are 
00197	flashing back to their childhoods when no one was trustworthy. Trust repair is an 
00198	essential process in healing the attachment disorders created by pervasive childhood 
00199	trauma. PTSD clients do not have a volitional "on" switch for trust, even though their 
00200	"off" switch is frequently automatically triggered during flashbacks. The therapist 
00201	therefore needs to be prepared to work on reassurance and trust restoral over and over 
00202	again. I have heard too many client stories about past therapists who got angry at 
00203	them because they would not simply choose to trust them. 
00204	The therapist therefore needs to be prepared to work on reassurance and trust 
00205	restoral over and over again. I have heard too many client stories about past 
00206	therapists who got angry at them because they would not simply choose to 
00207	trust them. 
00208	Retuning to the above vignette, I wondered out loud to my client, "Do you think you 
00209	might be in a flashback?" Because of the numerous times we had previously identified 
00210	and named her current type of experience as an emotional flashback, she immediately 
00211	recognized this and let go into deep sobbing. She dropped into profound grieving that 
00212	allowed her to release the flashback—a type of grieving the r estorative power of which 
00213	I have witnessed innumerable times. It is a crying that combines tears of relief with 
00214	tears of grief: relief at being able to take in another's empathy and make sense of 
00215	confusing, overwhelming pain; and grief over the childhood abandonment that created 
00216	this sense of abject alienation in the first place.  
00217	My client released some of the pain of her original trauma and of the times she had 
00218	previously been stuck in the unrelenting pain of flashing back to her original 
00219	abandonment. As her tears subsided, she recalled to me a time as a small child when 
00220	she had literally received a single lump of coal in her Christmas stocking as punishment 
00221	for being ten minutes late to dinner. Her tears morphed into healthy anger about this 
00222	abuse, and she felt herself returning to an empowered sense of self. Grieving brought 
00223	her back into the present and broke the amnesia of the flashback. She could then 
00224	remember to invoke the self-protective resources we had gradually been building in her 
00225	therapy with role-plays, assertiveness training and psychoeducation about her parents' 
00226	destruction of her healthy instinct to defend herself against abuse and unfairness. The 
00227	ubiquitous childhood phrase of "That's not fair!" had been severely punished and 
00228	extinguished by her parents. She reconnected with her right and need to have 
00229	boundaries, to judge her parents' actions unconscionable, and to fiercely say "no" to 
00230	her critic's subsequent habit of judging her harshly for every peccadillo. Finally, I 
00231	reminded her to reinvoke her sense of safety by recognizing that she now inhabited an 
00232	adult body, free of parental control, and that she had many resources to draw on: 
00233	intelligence, strength, resilience, and a growing sense of community. She lived in a safe 
00234	home; she had the support of her therapist and two friends who were her allies and 
00235	who readily saw her essential worth. I also observed that she was making ongoing 
00236	progress in managing her flashbacks–that they were occurring less often and less 
00237	intensely.

00243	Managing the Inner Critic 
00244	In guiding clients to develop their ability to manage emotional flashbacks, my most 
00245	common intervention involves helping them to deconstruct the alarmist tendencies of 
00246	the inner critic. This is essential, as Donald Kalshed explains in The Inner World of 
00247	Trauma, because the inner critic grows rampantly in traumatized children, and because 
00248	the inner critic not only exacerbates flashbacks, but eventually grows into a psychic 
00249	agency that initiates them. Continuous abuse and neglect force the child's inner critic 
00250	(superego) to overdevelop perfectionism and hypervigilance. The perfectionism of 
00251	Complex PTSD puts the child's every thought, word or action on trial and judges her as 
00252	fatally flawed if any of them are not one hundred percent faultless. Perfectionism then 
00253	devolves into the child's obsessive attempt to root out real or imagined defects and to 
00254	achieve unsurpassable excellence in an effort to win a modicum of safety and 
00255	comforting attachment.  
00256	The hypervigilance of Complex PTSD is an overaroused sympathetic nervous system 
00257	fixation on endangerment that comes from long-term childhood exposure to real 
00258	danger. In an effort to recognize, predict and avoid danger, hypervigilance develops in 
00259	a traumatized child as an incessant, on-guard scanning of both the real environment 
00260	and, most especially, the imagined upcoming environment. Hypervigilance typically 
00261	devolves into intense performance anxiety on every level of self-expression, and 
00262	perfectionism festers into a virulent inner voice that manifests as self-hate, self-disgust 
00263	and self-abandonment at every turn.  
00264	When the child with Complex PTSD eventually comes of age and launches from the 
00265	traumatizing family, she is so dominated by feelings of danger, shame and 
00266	abandonment that she is often unaware that adulthood now offers many new resources 
00267	for achieving internal and external safety and healthy connection with others. She is 
00268	unaware that a huge part of her identity is subsumed in the inner critic--the proxy of 
00269	her dysfunctional caregivers--and that she has had scarce room to develop a healthy 
00270	self with an accompanying healthy ego. 
00271	This scenario arises frequently in my practice: A client, in the midst of reporting some 
00272	inconsequential miscue of the previous week, suddenly launches into a catastrophizing 
00273	tale of her life deteriorating into a cascading series of disasters. She is flashing back to 
00274	the danger-ridden times of her childhood, and her distress sounds something like this: 
00275	"My boss looked at me funny when I came back from my bathroom break this morning 
00276	and I know he thinks I'm stupid and lazy and is going to fire me. I just know I won't be 
00277	able to get another job. My boyfriend will think I'm a loser and leave me. I'll get sick 
00278	from the stress, and with no money to pay my medical insurance and rent, I'll soon be 
00279	a bag lady on the street." It's disturbing how many catastrophizing inner critic rants 
00280	end with the bag lady on the street. What a symbol of abandonment!  
00281	It's disturbing how many catastrophizing inner critic rants end with the bag 
00282	lady on the street. What a symbol of abandonment!  
00283	Teaching such clients to recognize when they have polarized into inner-critic 
00284	catastrophizing, and modeling to them how to resist it with thought stopping and 
00285	thought substitution, are essential steps in managing flashbacks. In this case I 
00286	reminded my client of the many times we had previously caught the inner critic 
00287	laundry-listing every conceivable way a difficult situation could spiral into disaster, and 
00288	I invited her to use thought stopping to refuse to indulge this process. I suggested that 
00289	she visualize a stop sign and say "no" to the critic each time it tried to scare or demean 
00290	her. I reminded her that she had learned to catastrophize from her parents, who 
00291	noticed her in such a predominantly negative and intimidating way. I also reinvoked the 
00292	thought substitution process we had practiced on numerous occasions, encouraging her 
00293	to remember and focus on all the positive things she knew about herself. Finally, I 
00294	reminded her of all the positive experiences she had actually had with her boss, and I 
00295	listed the essential qualities and accomplishments we were working to integrate into 
00296	her self-image: her intelligence, integrity, resilience, kindness, and many successes at 
00297	work and school. 
00298	Rescuing the Wounded Child 
00299	Over the course of a therapy, I often reframe emotional flashbacks as messages from 
00300	the wounded inner child designed to challenge denial or minimization about childhood 
00301	trauma. It is as if the inner child is clamoring for validation of past parental abuse and 
00302	neglect: "See this is how bad it was--how overwhelmed, terrified, ashamed and 
00303	abandoned I felt so much of the time." When seen in this light, emotional flashbacks 
00304	are also signals from the wounded child that many of her developmental needs have 
00305	not been met. Most important among these are the needs for safety and for 
00306	Winnicottian good-enough attachment. There are no needs more important than those 
00307	of a parent's protection and empathy, without which a child cannot own and develop 
00308	her instincts for self-protection and self-compassion—the cornerstones of a healthy 
00309	ego. Without awakening to the need for this kind of primal self-advocacy, clients 
00310	remain stuck in learned self-abandonment and rarely develop effective resistance to 
00311	internal or external abuse, and seldom gain the motivation to consistently use the 13 
00312	tools for managing emotional flashbacks at the end of this article. 
00313	Emotional flashbacks are also signals from the wounded child that many of her 
00314	developmental needs have not been met. Most important among these are the 
00315	needs for safety and for Winnicottian good-enough attachment. 
00316	When clients recognize that their emotional storms are messages from an inner child 
00317	who is still pining for a healthy inner attachment figure, and when they are able to 
00318	internalize the therapist's acceptance and support, they gradually become more self-
00319	accepting and less ashamed of their flashbacks, their imperfections and their dysphoric 
00320	affective experience. When the therapist repeatedly models feeling-based indignation at 
00321	the fact that the client was taught to hate himself, the client eventually feels incensed 
00322	enough about this experience to begin standing up to the inner critic and of investing in 
00323	the extensive work of building healthy self-advocacy. When the therapist consistently 
00324	responds compassionately to the client's suffering, the client's capacity for self-
00325	empathy and self-forgiveness begins to awaken. He gradually begins to desire to 
00326	comfort and soothe himself in times of cognitive confusion, emotional pain, physical 
00327	distress, or real-life disappointment, rather than surrender to an inner torture of self-
00328	hate, self-disappointment, and self-abandonment. 
00329	Around this time in therapy, the client also solidifies her understanding that the lion's 
00330	share of the energy contained in her intense emotional flashbacks are actually 
00331	appropriate but delayed reactions to various themes of her childhood abuse and 
00332	neglect. Gradually—often at the rate of two steps forward and one back –-she is able to 
00333	metabolize these feelings in a way that not only resolves her trauma, but builds new, 
00334	healthy, self-empowering psychic structure as well. This, in turn, leads to an ongoing 
00335	reduction of the unresolved psychic pain that fuels her emotional flashbacks, which 
00336	subsequently become less frequent, intense and enduring. Eventually, a person 
00337	experiencing an emotional flashback begins to invoke a sense of self-protection as soon 
00338	as she realizes she is triggered, or even immediately upon being triggered. As 
00339	flashbacks decrease and become more manageable, the defensive structures built 
00340	around them (narcissistic, obsessive-compulsive, dissociative and/or codependent) can 
00341	be more readily deconstructed. 
00342	Moving through Abandonment into Intimacy: A Case Study 
00343	A sweet, middle-aged male client of mine from an upper-middle-class family had 
00344	suffered severe emotional abandonment in childhood. Both parents were workaholics 
00345	and therefore unavailable; as the youngest of five children, my client was hamstrung in 
00346	the sibling competition for scarce parental resources. His adulthood reenacted the 
00347	relational impoverishment of childhood. He was hair-triggered for retreat and isolation. 
00348	He had never experienced an enduring relationship. As a result of our long-term work, 
00349	however, he became more motivated to seek a relationship, and successfully dated a 
00350	healthy and available partner. For the first six months of their relationship, her kind 
00351	nature, along with my coaching, enabled him to show her more and more of himself, 
00352	and he was rewarded by increasing feelings of comfort and love while relating with her.  
00353	When he accepted her request to move in together, however, it became harder to hide 
00354	his recurring emotional flashbacks to the overwhelming anxiety and emptiness of his 
00355	childhood. He was more convinced than ever that the abandonment melange of fear, 
00356	shame and depression at the core of his flashbacks was the most despicable of his 
00357	many fatal flaws. As we worked with this belief in therapy, he remembered many times 
00358	when even the mildest dip in his mood triggered his psychotherapist mother to turn her 
00359	back on him and flee to the inviolability of her locked room. He saw that the occasional 
00360	utility his mother found in him depended on his keeping her buoyant and lifting her 
00361	spirits. He was traumatized into a staunch conviction that social inclusion depended on 
00362	his manifesting a bravura of love, listening and entertainment. A codependent defense 
00363	of fawning and performing had been instilled in him. Now he could not shake off the 
00364	fear that if he ever deviated from being loving, funny and bright, his new partner would 
00365	be disgusted and abandon him. He reported that, in fact, his flashbacks at home had 
00366	increased, provoking a desperate need to isolate and hide. His freeze response was 
00367	activated and he increasingly disappeared from her into silence, the computer, 
00368	excessive sleeping, and marathon TV sports viewing. During his most intense 
00369	flashbacks, his fear and self-disgust became so intense that his flight response took 
00370	over and he invented any excuse to get out of the house. He was besieged by thoughts 
00371	and fantasies of being single again. His inner critic was winning the battle; he was sure 
00372	his partner was as disgusted with his affect as his mother had been. He was on the 
00373	verge of a full-fledged flight response into the old habit of precipitously ending 
00374	relationships, as he always had in the past when the brief infatuation stages of his few 
00375	previous relationships came to an end. 
00376	During his most intense flashbacks, his fear and self-disgust became so 
00377	intense that his flight response took over and he invented any excuse to get

00380	We spent many subsequent sessions managing these emotional flashbacks to his 
00381	original abandonment. He understood more deeply that his silent withdrawals were 
00382	evidence that he was flashing back, and he committed to rereading and using the 13 
00383	steps of flashback management at such times. With my encouragement and gentle 
00384	nudging, he grieved over his original abandonment more deeply and more self-
00385	compassionately than ever before in our work together. Over and over, he confronted 
00386	the critic's projection of his mother onto his partner. He practiced grounding himself in 
00387	the present, and at home began talking to his girlfriend about his experiences of 
00388	flashing back into the abandonment melange. A crowning achievement occurred when 
00389	he was finally able to disclose to her that talking vulnerably made him feel even more 
00390	afraid and ashamed—and deserving of abandonment.  
00391	To his great relief, he was rewarded not only by her empathic response but also by her 
00392	gratitude for his vulnerability, and she began to share an even deeper level of her own 
00393	vulnerability. For the first time, he began talking to her while he was actually 
00394	depressed. Their love then began to expand into those special depths of intimacy that 
00395	are only achieved when people feel safe enough to communicate about all of their 
00396	cognitive, emotional and behavioral experiences--the good and the bad, the gratifying 
00397	and the disappointing, the loving and the mad. (One of the great rewards of this kind of 
00398	recovery work is that the individual achieves a depth and richness of communication 
00399	and contact that many non-traumatized people miss out on because wider social forces 
00400	have scared and shamed them out of ever sharing anything truly vulnerable.) As my 
00401	client became more skilled at being vulnerable, he was rewarded with the irreplaceable 
00402	intimacy that comes from commiseration—another gift that many less- traumatized 
00403	members of our culture never get to discover. The degree to which two individuals 
00404	mutually share all aspects of their experience is the degree to which they have real love 
00405	and intimacy. 
00406	As clients learn to identify flashbacks as normal responses to abnormally stressful 
00407	childhood conditions, they become free of the fear and shame that have made them 
00408	isolate, overreact, or push others away at such times. Most clients experience 
00409	tremendous relief when they learn to interpret their overwhelming or excessively 
00410	numbing experiences as emotional flashbacks, rather then as proof that they are bad, 
00411	defective, worthless or crazy. Such realizations–-as rapidly evaporating as they can be 
00412	in early recovery--heal the fear and shame so central to emotional flashbacks. As 
00413	clients learn to stay in contact and communicate functionally from their pain, they 
00414	begin to heal their core abandonment depression; they gradually discover that they are 
00415	not detestable but lovable and acceptable in their deepest vulnerability. This begins to 
00416	heal their attachment disorders, the most deleterious part of Complex PTSD. It allows 
00417	them to evolve toward what some traumatologists call an earned secure attachment. 
00418	For many people this first secure attachment is achieved with the therapist, which in 
00419	turn allows the client to know that such an invaluable experience is possible. With 
00420	ongoing psychoeducation and coaching from the therapist, this first safe-enough 
00421	relationship can become the launching pad for seeking such a relationship outside of 
00422	therapy. The ending phase of therapy is typically characterized by the client building at 
00423	least one good-enough, earned secure attachment outside of therapy-–one relationship 
00424	where she has learned to manage her flashbacks without excessively acting out against 
00425	others or herself.  
00426	Challenges and Rewards for the Therapist  
00427	What I find most difficult about this work is that it is often excruciatingly slow and 
00428	gradual. Nowhere is this truer than in the work of shrinking the toxic inner critic. 
00429	Progress is often beyond the perception of the client, especially during a flashback, and 
00430	flashbacks are unfortunately never completely arrested. The hardest thing of all is 
00431	getting the client to see that emotional flashbacks, a bit like diabetes, are a lifetime 
00432	condition that will always need a modicum of management. Good-enough management 
00433	creates a good-enough life--one where flashbacks markedly and continually decrease 
00434	but inevitably recur from time to time. Failure to accept this reality typically causes the 
00435	client to reinvoke her old reactions to flashbacks, which in turn cause her to get lost in 
00436	the self-abandonment of blaming and shaming herself. 
00437	The hardest thing of all is getting the client to see that emotional flashbacks, a 
00438	bit like diabetes, are a lifetime condition that will always need a modicum of 
00439	management. 
00440	What I love most about this kind of trauma work is seeing clients with a long history of 
00441	developmental arrest, as well as feelings of helplessness and hopelessness, begin to 
00442	become empowered. I am delighted every time a client responds to her own suffering 
00443	with kindness or reports an action of self-protectiveness in the world at large. I love 
00444	witnessing the gradual growth of self-confidence and self-expression in my clients. This 
00445	inevitably seems to grow out of their recovered ability to get angry about what 
00446	happened to them in childhood and to use that anger to empower and motivate 
00447	themselves to face the fear of trying on new, more assertive behavior. I am also 
00448	especially moved when a client learns to cry for himself in that fully functional, 
00449	unabashed way where tears release fear and shame. In my experience, nothing 
00450	catharsizes fear and catastrophizing obsessiveness like egosyntonic tears. I have, on 
00451	thousands of occasions, witnessed clients grieving in a way that resurrects them from a 
00452	flashback, back into their growing self-esteem and resourcefulness.  
00453	Another highlight of this work for me comes in the early and middle stages of therapy. I 
00454	like to call it rescuing the client from the hegemony of the critic. I believe there is an 
00455	unmet childhood need for rescue that I help meet when I "save" my client from the 
00456	critic—unlike Mom who didn't save him from his abusive dad, or unlike the 
00457	neighborhood that didn't rescue him from his alcoholic family. Decades of trauma work 
00458	have taken me to a place where my heart no longer allows me to be silent, and hence 
00459	tacitly approving, when clients verbally and emotionally abuse themselves in a gross 
00460	overidentification with the inner critic. I am additionally motivated to do this because of 
00461	the failure of my own first long-term experience of psychoanalytic therapy, where my 
00462	"blank screen" therapist let me flounder and perseverate in endless iterations of my 
00463	PTSD-acquired self-hate and self-disgust. Never once was it pointed out that I could 
00464	and should challenge this anti-self behavior. UCSF trauma expert Harvey Peskin would 
00465	call this a failure to bear witness to the traumatization of the child. I have learned to 
00466	take this a step further by not only vocally witnessing the client's flashback into the 
00467	helplessness of his original abandonment, but also giving him a hand to climb out of 
00468	that abyss of fear and shame.  
00469	The term rescuing and what it represents has become a taboo in the 12-Step 
00470	Movement (e.g. Alcoholics Anonymous, Adult Children of Alcoholics, Incest Survivors 
00471	Anonymous, etc.) and many psychotherapy circles. The word "rescuing" is often used in 
00472	such an all-or-none way that any type of active helping is pathologized. However, I 
00473	think helping clients out of the abyss of emotional flashbacks is a necessary form of 
00474	active helping, or rescuing. The rescuing I refer to is different from the kind that many 
00475	therapists correctly view as disempowering and unhealthy for the client. One example 
00476	of this type of countertherapeutic rescuing is inappropriate or excessive advocacy. 
00477	Colluding with or encouraging personal irresponsibility, such as exonerating a client's 
00478	regressed or infantile acting out without steering him towards learning to interact more 
00479	responsibly and salubriously with himself and the world is also a common type of 
00480	problematic rescuing. 
00481	A final great reward I experience in helping clients manage their emotional flashbacks 
00482	is witnessing the development of their emotional and relational intelligence. At the risk 
00483	of sounding Pollyannaish, I believe Complex PTSD actually has a silver lining: the 
00484	potential to reconnect with these intelligences at much deeper levels than those who 
00485	are not traumatized in the family, but who suffer a truncation of their emotional self-
00486	expression and relational capacity. Wider social forces can strand individuals in the 
00487	loneliness of superficial relating and can cause them to hide significant aspects of their 
00488	emotional experience. A number of my clients in the later stages of recovery work have 
00489	built and earned relationships that exhibit a depth of intimacy I rarely see in the 
00490	general population.

00493	MANAGING EMOTIONAL FLASHBACKS  
00494	1. Say to yourself: "I am having a flashback." Flashbacks take us into a 
00495	timeless part of the psyche that feels as helpless, hopeless and surrounded 
00496	by danger as we were in childhood. The feelings and sensations you are 
00497	experiencing are past memories that cannot hurt you now. 
00498	2. Remind yourself: "I feel afraid but I am not in danger! I am safe 
00499	now, here in the present." Remember you are now in the safety of the 
00500	present, far from the danger of the past. 
00501	3. Own your right/need to have boundaries. Remind yourself that you 
00502	do not have to allow anyone to mistreat you; you are free to leave 
00503	dangerous situations and protest unfair behavior.  
00504	4. Speak reassuringly to your Inner Child. The child needs to know that 
00505	you love her unconditionally– that she can come to you for comfort and 
00506	protection when she feels lost and scared.  
00507	5. Deconstruct eternity thinking. In childhood, fear and abandonment 
00508	felt endless—a safer future was unimaginable. Remember the flashback will 
00509	pass as it has many times before. 
00510	6. Remind yourself that you are in an adult body with allies, skills and 
00511	resources to protect you that you never had as a child. (Feeling small and 
00512	little is a sure sign of a flashback.) 
00513	7. Ease back into your body. Fear launches us into "heady" worrying, or 
00514	numbing and spacing out.  
00515	 Gently ask your body to relax. Feel each of your major muscle 
00516	groups and softly encourage them to relax. (Tightened musculature 
00517	sends unnecessary danger signals to the brain.)  
00518	 Breathe deeply and slowly. (Holding the breath also signals 
00519	danger.)  
00520	 Slow down. Rushing presses the psyche's panic button. 
00521	 Find a safe place to unwind and soothe yourself: wrap yourself in a 
00522	blanket, hold a stuffed animal, lie down in a closet or a bath, take a 
00523	nap.  
00524	 Feel the fear in your body without reacting to it. Fear is just an 
00525	energy in your body that cannot hurt you if you do not run from it or 
00526	react self-destructively to it. 
00527	8. Resist the Inner Critic's catastrophizing. (a) Use thought-stopping 
00528	to halt its exaggeration of danger and need to control the uncontrollable. 
00529	Refuse to shame, hate or abandon yourself. Channel the anger of self-attack 
00530	into saying no to unfair self-criticism. (b) Use thought-substitution to 
00531	replace negative thinking with a memorized list of your qualities and 
00532	accomplishments. 
00533	9. Allow yourself to grieve. Flashbacks are opportunities to release old, 
00534	unexpressed feelings of fear, hurt, and abandonment, and to validate—and 
00535	then soothe—the child's past experience of helplessness and hopelessness. 
00536	Healthy grieving can turn our tears into self-compassion and our anger into 
00537	self-protection.  
00538	10. Cultivate safe relationships and seek support. Take time alone 
00539	when you need it, but don't let shame isolate you. Feeling shame doesn't 
00540	mean you are shameful. Educate those close to you about flashbacks and 
00541	ask them to help you talk and feel your way through them. 
00542	11. Learn to identify the types of triggers that lead to flashbacks. 
00543	Avoid unsafe people, places, activities and triggering mental processes. 
00544	Practice preventive maintenance with these steps when triggering situations 
00545	are unavoidable.  
00546	12. Figure out what you are flashing back to. Flashbacks are 
00547	opportunities to discover, validate and heal our wounds from past abuse and 
00548	abandonment. They also point to our still-unmet developmental needs and 
00549	can provide motivation to get them met. 
00550	13. Be patient with a slow recovery process. It takes time in the 
00551	present to become un-adrenalized, and considerable time in the future to 
00552	gradually decrease the intensity, duration and frequency of flashbacks. Real 
00553	recovery is a gradual process—often two steps forward, one step back. Don't 
00554	beat yourself up for having a flashback.
