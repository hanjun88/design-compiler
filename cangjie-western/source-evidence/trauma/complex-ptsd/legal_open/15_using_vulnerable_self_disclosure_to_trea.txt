00005	    Along with many other tools, I use Vulnerable Self-Disclosure [VSD] to address the relational 
00006	developmental arrests that are typical in Complex PTSD [CPTSD]. CPTSD is also now known as 
00007	Developmental Trauma Disorder [DTD]. Perhaps the two most dire developmental arrests of CPTSD are 
00008	the lack of a supportive relationship with oneself and the lack of a supportive relationship with anyone 
00009	else. 
00010	    Ongoing childhood trauma destroys self-esteem and the capacity to feel safe and sustained by others. 
00011	Traumatic parenting turns the superego in the developing child into a totalitarian inner critic that spoils 
00012	inner and outer relating with relational perfectionism. Continuous parental abuse, criticism and/or 
00013	neglect also installs chronic toxic shame and intense social anxiety, both of which ruin relating to oneself 
00014	and to others.  
00015	    I use VSD to help survivors become more compassionate with themselves. This in turn gradually 
00016	awakens an innate capacity to relate healthily to themselves and safe enough others. I will illustrate this 
00017	below with five vignettes. 
00018	    [This article expands my paper, “Emotional Flashback Management in Treating Complex PTSD”, first 
00019	published on Psychoterapy.net, 9/2009.]   
00020	A Vignette Illustrating Vulnerable Self-Disclosure     
00021	    I have been working weekly for six months with 48-year-old Jane, a newly licensed CBT 
00022	psychotherapist, who believes that her cognitions should trump her emotions. She is often cruelly 
00023	exasperated with herself for her unending struggles with anxiety and depression. “What’s wrong with 
00024	me!? Why don’t I just stop feeling this way? Why can’t I decide not to surrender to these traps? I’m such 
00025	a loser!  I can’t even master simple thought-stopping. What kind of screwed up person won’t even help 
00026	themselves when They Know What To Do! How dare I pretend to be a therapist!?” 
00027	    Jane is also reading my book, Complex PTSD, and waveringly thinks that her suffering springs from a 
00028	childhood-induced perfectionism that she may or may not want to renounce. Periodically she jams on 
00029	the pronouncement: “Perfectionism has actually helped me a lot!” 
00030	    Today she says: “Pete, I’ve been thinking about your theory that my shame and anxiety come from my 
00031	parents always looking at me in a negative light. I just can’t see how my perfectionism comes from that. 
00032	My parents weren’t that bad!” 
00033	    Jane drifts into thought and then continues: “I know what I just said but, I can’t stop seeing them as 
00034	‘The King and Queen of Negative-Noticing’. It’s awful but I have no memories of them seeing me in a 
00035	positive light. Maybe that IS why I have this intense inner critic. I mean, when I was a kid, a ninety-nine 
00036	on a test would typically be greeted with a scornful lecture on the missing one percent!” 
00037	    I expand on this: “You know I think that when kids are constantly criticized, they are at risk of 
00038	developing CPTSD. Being assailed continuously with a jaundiced view can make you adopt that stance as 
00039	a belief. The outer ‘You’re bad’ voice eventually becomes an inner ‘I’m bad’ voice…and it never lets up”  
00040	    “Yeah!? I don’t know, Pete! I really am pretty bad. I’m a therapist and I can’t even help myself.”  
00041	    “Well, Jane, I believe that is your inner critic talking now. It’s your internalized parents doing a number 
00042	on you. Speaking from my own experience, I still sometimes jump on myself when I’m in an emotional 
00043	flashback…I feel as ashamed and scared as if my mother was yelling at me with the disgust that used to 
00044	pour out of her regularly.” 
00045	     “Yeah, I get that” says Jane. “I mean I actually experience that a lot. I’m kind of glad I’m not the only 
00046	one, but I still know that my parents really loved me.” 
00047	    I pause and change tack: “Yes, I bet that they thought they were being loving…doing it for your own 
00048	good. And, I apologize for all the psychoeducating…I guess I’m a bit stuck on some studies that show 
00049	that kids who get a higher ratio of positive-to-negative attention develop better self-esteem.” 
00050	    A moment of silence, and then Jane says: “Well, OK, I actually do get that! Them ALWAYS being so 
00051	hard on me does look like how I’m ALWAYS so hard on myself. Even though it freaks me out to hear 
00052	myself say it: ‘I pretty much hate myself all the time!’”  
00053	     A short silence and she becomes teary…and then angry, and says: “I wish you could have heard my 
00054	mother on the phone last night. She was so awful! Beyond nasty! Really! Part of me secretly calls her 
00055	‘The Queen of Dishing-It-Out’! God! What a disloyal little brat I am! But, OH! she went on and on about 
00056	how ungrateful I am.”  
00057	    Jane becomes silent for a moment ,and then bursts out imitating her mother’s litany of slurs. Her 
00058	mother’s toxic laundry list then seamlessly shifts into Jane resuming her own self-mocking.  
00059	    Feeling sad for her, I interrupt her and gently point out that she is scorning herself with the exact 
00060	same phrases and disgusted emotional tone that she just quoted from her mother. Her eyes moisten. 
00061	    I shift focus and wonder out loud: “Can you feel…call up…some sympathy for that scared little girl who 
00062	could never protest all that unfair…and dead wrong criticism…that little girl who spent so much time in 
00063	the abyss of anxious shame and depression that your mother and father created…?”  
00064	    She cuts me off: “Dammit! I can’t do anything right! You’ve given me this permission over and over, 
00065	and I just refuse to be kind to myself! How can you even bear to be in the same room with me? I know I 
00066	make people sick. How’d you get so good at hiding your disgust!?” 
00067	    I feel a tremor of fear about her anger, but much more a tsunami of compassion for the horribly 
00068	scapegoated child that she obviously was. It’s so sad that little children can be taught to hate themselves 
00069	so mercilessly.  
00070	    I decide to let my naturally occurring compassion become visible. I’ve been holding back my teariness 
00071	since she self-disgustedly echoed her mother on the phone. But, now I lessen my resistance. I let my 
00072	spontaneous tears about her pain well up in my eyes, without spilling, as a window into my caring. 
00073	    These are the same tears I often have while empathizing with a sweet character in a movie who is 
00074	being unfairly bullied -- the same compassionate tears that I have cried for the little boy in me a 
00075	thousand times when I have been stuck in a memory of being verbally eviscerated by my mother -- the 
00076	same tears that have released me from hundreds of overwhelming emotional flashbacks, ever since I 
00077	learned to cry in self-compassion for my own pain.  
00078	    Welling up is my most therapeutic self-disclosive tool. I “got permission” to use it from my own 
00079	therapist who did the same with me decades ago, and instantly pumped my trust up to a level I had 
00080	never experienced before. 
00081	    I can see she’s taken aback and puzzled about how to respond. So, I say: “You know, you’re right, 
00082	Jane. I have been hiding something. But it’s not disgust. It’s my instinctual compassion for you. It’s so 
00083	sad to see you beat yourself up so viciously. I feel like I can see you as a little girl being bullied and 
00084	picked on over and over by your scowling parents. I see how you had to parrot their toxic perspective of 
00085	you to survive. You learned to beat them to the punch and endlessly echo their criticisms.” 
00086	    Jane begins to tear up. Her body seems to relax and open up in receptivity.  
00087	    I continue: “I feel sad when you bully yourself just like they did. I feel sad that there was no one 
00088	around to tell you how awful and destructive they were being. You were an innocent child. Trying so 
00089	hard to please them. No child deserves such treatment. No child. Not a single one! They Had No Right To 
00090	Be So Mean To You!” I let a trace of anger temper the last sentence, modelling a healthy protective 
00091	emotional response to bad parenting behavior. 
00092	    And Glory Halleluiah! For the first time, Jane bursts into tears. After a really good cry, she says: “I 
00093	don’t know why I’m crying? I must have deserved it! But it also feels good to hear you blame them. And I 
00094	do kind of feel sorry for the little girl that I was. Especially when she was so little…and SOO Loving! I 
00095	mean I am a loving person! That’s why I became a therapist!” 
00096	    Wow that hit the spot, and here comes another lovely, releasing cloudburst of tears. 
00097	    Her tears ebb and Jane says: “I think I’m beginning to get it! They really were abnormally mean. When 
00098	I got off the phone with my mother last night, I got this image, that’s also been rather intrusive lately: 
00099	she and dad as a mean TV tag-team wresting duo.  
00100	    “Hmmm, maybe these sessions, and you sharing how much your parents were like mine, are helping 
00101	me to see that…perhaps, it wasn’t all my fault. And maybe I did go overboard in trying to be perfect to 
00102	get them to like me and stop criticizing me. Maybe I can learn to stop imitating them!”  
00103	    Many more tears come.  
00104	    As this wave subsides, she says: “Oh my God, I have this awful image. THEY PUSHED ME 
00105	OVERBOARD…And I never got back on board of the good strong ship that is me! I’m still desperately 
00106	treading water in that ocean of depression and fear  that they pushed me into!” 
00107	    “Well Jane, I hope this isn’t too much too soon, but…through no fault of your own…you got a bad 
00108	hand from the parenting deck. Every child deserves copious love, and encouragement. Your parents 
00109	really were derelict in their duty, and I hope we can use this experience to help you grow more 
00110	compassion for how badly you were treated…and that we can grow that compassion into some inner 
00111	self-protection against the process they indoctrinated you into of only noticing yourself in a negative 
00112	way.”   
00113	Vulnerable Self-Disclosure [VSD] 
00114	    For two decades, most of my clients have sought my services in response to reading my books and 
00115	website articles about Complex PTSD [www.pete-walker.com]. Many have told me that the vulnerable 
00116	self-disclosures with which I illustrate recovery principles, helped them to overcome their fear and 
00117	shame about seeking help.  
00118	     I vividly remember a quavering voice on the phone: “I know this sounds dumb, but I’m terribly scared 
00119	that I trust you…you might even be able to help me.” I thanked her for her vulnerable honesty and 
00120	reverberated: “I commend you on your bravery. I remember how hard and scary it was for me to seek 
00121	therapy…not to mention, show up week after week feeling so vulnerable.” Later, well into the therapy, 
00122	she said: “You telling me that helped me so much to continue this long difficult journey, especially on 
00123	the many occasions that I felt like giving up!” 
00124	    
00125	    Grateful feedback from trauma survivors continuously validates my use of VSD to ameliorate 
00126	relational trauma. Now whether or not someone has read my book, I judiciously and sparingly share my 
00127	own experiences of dealing egosyntonically with issues that still plague them.  
00128	    In so doing, I typically reveal parallel bits from my own trauma history in a self-accepting way. 
00129	Commonly, this gradually reduces the pervasive self-blame that survivors were taught to hold towards 
00130	their trauma symptoms. Eriksen said: “shame is blame turned against the self”. Tragically, traumatizing 
00131	parents teach survivors to blame themselves by shaming, attacking and emotionally abandoning them 
00132	for their imperfections, miscues, taboo feelings and expressions of need.  
00133	    Unconsciously hoping to gain safety and approval, such children adopt perfectionism as a coping 
00134	strategy. To do so they copy their parents by endlessly attacking and shaming themselves for every less-
00135	than perfect-aspect of themselves. Worthless”, “disgusting”, “appalling”, “fatally flawed” are very 
00136	common, deeply ingrained self-identifications in CPTSD. 
00137	     
00138	    Survivors who have been motivated by the vulnerability of my writing frequently dive quickly into our 
00139	work together and gradually learn to recognize, resist and shrink their toxic shame -- and its conjoined 
00140	twin, perfectionism.  
00141	    Once again, this is not simple, keeping-your-house-immaculate perfectionism. This is the one that 
00142	demands perfect thinking, feeling, performing, interacting, etc. I encourage survivors to fight this 
00143	impossible mandate and to grieve and vent vulnerably about being denied self-esteem…about never 
00144	being seen as good enough. 
00145	    To motivate them, I occasionally share that much of my release from toxic shame and chronic anxiety 
00146	came from embracing my grief…from allowing myself to cry and anger self-compassionately about my 
00147	parents’ traumatizing abuse and neglect. 
00148	    On other occasions, I sometimes model how I come to my own aid when I am, like them, circling the 
00149	drain of toxic shame during an emotional flashback. One older man, who I worked with for years, once 
00150	reported cheerfully to me that: “Thanks to learning your favorite tool of using your anger to set 
00151	boundaries with your critic, my toxic shame is a mere shadow of its former self. I finally got how that old 
00152	saying: ‘The beatings will continue until morale improves’ is the Catch 22 of the inner critic.” 
00153	       
00154	    Therapeutic self-disclosure is rightfully controversial. It can and too often does devolve into 
00155	narcissistic gratification. This can make VSD a slippery slope, yet there is a safe level ground where it is 
00156	exquisitely therapeutic. We do not need to throw the baby of soothing commiseration out with the 
00157	bathwater of countertherapeutic narcissistic gratification. Guidelines for a judicious use of self-
00158	disclosure are therefore listed at the end of this article, and hopefully modelled in the vignettes. 
00159	Developing Self-Compassion, Openness to Empathy & Mutuality with VSD 
00160	    To address arrested relational-development, I focus on awakening three instinctual processes that are 
00161	essential to healthy relating. These emotionally-based processes are Self-Compassion, Openness to 
00162	Empathy, and Mutuality [mutual supportiveness in intimate relationships].  
00163	    Here is an outline of my three-stage model for developing these three emotionally-based 
00164	characteristics of relational intelligence.  
00165	    1. During the first stage, I help survivors reconnect with their innate Self-Compassion.  I guide them to 
00166	improve their relationships with themselves -- to awaken their inborn empathy for themselves. With 
00167	enough practice, self-compassion often naturally awakens the instinct of self-protection. As survivors 
00168	care more for themselves, they naturally want to stand up for themselves when they are being attacked 
00169	– internally or externally. Untraumatized toddlers demonstrate this compassion for themselves by 
00170	quickly and enthusiastically learning to say “No!” and “That’s not fair!” when someone mistreats them. 
00171	    2.   During the second stage, I help my clients cultivate their Openness to Empathy. CPTSD-inducing 
00172	parents rarely, or never, show empathy. Even worse, their punitive actions and words are often passed 
00173	off as love and care. Subsequently, caring actions from others can feel triggering, frightening and/or 
00174	phony. I use my vulnerability to inspire safety -- to open my clients to my comforting. Eventually this 
00175	allows them to trust that someone else can be truly supportive. 
00176	    3.   During the third stage, I work to develop Mutuality, i.e., mutual supportiveness in relationship. 
00177	Without mutuality, there is no true intimacy. Relationships are narcissistic when only one person is 
00178	supported. With enough growth in the first two stages, many survivors naturally experience feelings of 
00179	reciprocal empathy. Their arrested capacity to form intimate relationships based on reciprocal caring 
00180	begins to develop.   
00181	    At its most progressive, these three stages become interwoven. The client and I can then co-create a 
00182	blueprint of what attachment theorists call an earned secure attachment. In best case scenarios, a 
00183	secure attachment then manifests more fully with a friend or partner in the client’s outside life.  
00184	1. Guiding the Development of Self-Compassion     
00185	    If you Google: “Dr. Dan Siegel and Interpersonal Neurobiology”, you will find enlightening videos and 
00186	written material on the many scientific studies that show how essential compassion is for well-being and 
00187	psychological health.    
00188	    Vulnerable Self-Disclosure [VSD] is a potent tool for guiding survivors to fully accept themselves. warts 
00189	and all. VSD can guide them to forgive themselves for their mistakes [real, purported or imagined].  Over 
00190	time this helps deconstruct chronic negative self-noticing, and gradually replaces it with a self-
00191	perpetuating perspective of holding themselves in kindness.  
00192	    As a silver lining, they often learn to renounce the prevailing culture’s emotional perfectionism of 
00193	“Don’t Worry, Be Happy”. They increasingly realize that they do not have to be perpetually shiny or 
00194	performing at a hundred percent all the time to deserve love. More and more, they see themselves as 
00195	worthy of their own love, no matter what.  
00196	 
00197	     Like many effective therapists, I use my compassion to help my clients discover and develop their 
00198	own self-compassion. I consistently model kindness for my own dysphoric feelings and CPTSD 
00199	symptoms, and I always choose examples analogous to their current suffering. I must, of course, have 
00200	sufficiently “digested” these events. 
00201	    Here are some examples: “I caught that virus too. I feel kind of crappy today”; “Yeah, I feel for you. My 
00202	best friend moved out of town last year, and the loss of him living nearby, still recurringly makes me feel 
00203	sad”; “I hear you. What Trump said yesterday about women makes me wish him an especially bad hair 
00204	day!” 
00205	    Reverberating with a tone that demonstrates acceptance of my matching pain, helps authenticate my 
00206	compassion. Eventually, many clients realize that they can be sympathetic with themselves when they 
00207	are in pain.  
00208	    Here are a few more brief examples: “Yes, Carol, my mother was mean like yours, and Mother’s Day 
00209	sometimes feels like cruel mockery to me too!”; “Jim, I hate that he did that; bullies like your father and 
00210	mine really piss me off!”; “Yes, Tamika, I also once ran into the back of a car when I was spacing out! I 
00211	was so mortified…and scared!”; “You know, Ramon, I really get it. Waking up depressed is one of my 
00212	most unfavorite things! But these days, I am usually able to be especially kind to myself at such times.”  
00213	 
00214	    I have repeatedly found that apologizing for miscues in sessions is especially therapeutic. When I 
00215	apologize, I do so with a tone of mild sorrow and self-kindness to model that self-esteem can survive 
00216	mistakes. This also models healthy rapport-repair.  
00217	    Here are how my apologies sometimes sound: “I’m sorry Nancy, I really misheard you then; and I’d 
00218	really like to get it. Would you please explain it to me again?”; “I’m sorry I forgot that you told me that 
00219	already. I think my regret about it will help me hold onto that information.” 
00220	    Such interventions deepen a sense of safety. Often it helps clients to become increasingly disclosive of 
00221	their own flaws, and eventually forgiving of their own mistakes. Put another way, VSD can model that 
00222	human beings can be “good enough”, in the Winnicottian sense, despite their lapses, slip-ups and 
00223	imperfections 
00224	         
00225	    As survivors learn to grieve out their childhood pain with self-compassion, they increasingly feel relief; 
00226	they diminish their habits of self-attack and self-abandonment. Over time, deeply ingrained self-blame 
00227	[toxic shame] gives way to kindhearted self-forgiveness. Moreover, as self-compassion deepens, the 
00228	developmentally arrested instinct of self-protection often awakens.           
00229	    Tragically, most traumatizing parents punish children who protest being abused with especially 
00230	scathing vehemence. Children’s healthy assertiveness can be lost for their whole lives. The door that 
00231	blocks healthy self-protection, therefore, typically opens creakingly. I often use vulnerable self-
00232	disclosure to oil its hinges. The oil itself is my recovered anger – the healthy, innate feeling response to 
00233	unfairness, exploitation, hostility and short-shrift that is inborn in all humans.  
00234	    Here is a brief example of how I emotionally self-disclose to stimulate healthy self-protective anger: 
00235	“I’m feeling pissed off at your parents right now for teaching you to be so chronically mean to yourself. I 
00236	also hated myself for the same reason well into my forties.  
00237	    “Thankfully, I finally understood that my parents forced me to unfairly blame myself for everything, 
00238	even things that were clearly theirs or someone else’s fault.  More and more, as I angrily vented about 
00239	this, my shaming, self-blaming critic diminished.  
00240	    “So, I hope you’ll consider joining me in being mad at your parents for making you habitually turn your 
00241	anger against yourself. Let’s build the part of yourself that can ‘Just Say No’ to self-attack.” 
00242	A Vignette about Awakening Self-Compassion 
00243	    Liz and I have been working together for almost a year. Today she comes in reeling from being left by 
00244	her fourth partner in three years. She is drowning in shame, and berating herself mercilessly with a cat 
00245	o’ nine tails of her “fatal flaws”.  
00246	    “Why wouldn’t they leave me? Even I can’t stand myself. I’m such a loser…an ugly retard-loser. Oh 
00247	shit! Listen to me! I’m such a waste of space that I can’t even beat myself up right. ‘Retard’ is such a 
00248	politically incorrect word!” 
00249	    I have heard so much of this in past sessions that I could interrupt her and pick up her litany perfectly 
00250	like a parrot. Perish the thought! Instead, I opt to model compassionate self-care. “I’m sorry but I have 
00251	to stop you. I feel so sad hearing you imitating your parents. I feel like a witness to violent bullying who 
00252	is tacitly approving it by not doing anything to stop it. Your parents were so endlessly unkind to you, that 
00253	you were forced to join them in treating yourself the same way! 
00254	    “Liz, I know you well enough now to insist that you are good person with a good heart and a host of 
00255	other qualities. You don’t have to let your parents get away with brainwashing you into always blaming 
00256	yourself. That partner who just left you was your fourth narcissist in a row, and he scapegoated you 
00257	almost exactly like your parents did!” 
00258	    I let myself become teary…sad from both compassion for her and from remembering how I too used 
00259	to abandon myself so codependently to narcissists. I say: “It’s cataclysmically unfair, Liz, especially as 
00260	you’re such a loving person. It’s so ironic! You’re actually too loving…EXCEPT to yourself!” 
00261	     Ah! Pay dirt! Her eyes are welling…and here come her tears…and perhaps the chance to morph them 
00262	into self-compassion.  
00263	     I let her cry silently with a few soft encouraging “Yeses”. Suddenly, her face begins to scrunch up, and 
00264	it looks as if she is about to turn against her crying. 
00265	    I interrupt: “It’s okay, Liz. It’s good to cry. You’ve had a couple of good cries in our earlier sessions. 
00266	Those tears left you feeling relieved and on your own side. Let your tears come. You can’t cry too much 
00267	here…Yeah that’s it. That’s great. Let those tears invoke your own compassion for you, and for that 
00268	sweet little kid that suffered such painful parental rejection for so long.” 
00269	    She cries, and cries some more, and folds softly into herself, breathing deep and slow. After some 
00270	silence, I add: “It’s so sad how they set you up to fall in love with narcissists like them. You’ve had too 
00271	many partners who only take, who can never get enough…partners who hate and shame you whenever 
00272	they feel bad and need someone to take it out on. Just like you know who!” 
00273	    Her crying springs forth again from an even deeper place. I feel a vicarious sense of relief as her body 
00274	relaxes into it.  
00275	    Her tears ebb and she says: “I was just so lonely and scared all the time. I feel so sorry for that kid. I 
00276	wish I could go back and help her…tell her it wasn’t her fault.” 
00277	    For ten more minutes, she grieves and verbally vents out the pain of her own self-abandonment. “I’ve 
00278	just been so, so mean to myself.  Can I say I’m sorry to myself?” I nod. “I’m sorry, Liz!” She laughs: “That 
00279	sounded kind of weird, but it’s good to hear it! I think I get this self-compassion thing. I think I’ll say it a 
00280	few more times!” 
00281	    She laughs and cries some more. As she does this, as she gains more self-permission to befriend 
00282	herself, she then retreats into what looks like some very deep peace. 
00283	    I then disclose another aspect of what I am feeling. “Liz, please let me know if this is not okay, but I 
00284	feel I have to let you know that I just feel really pissed off at your parents right now. Some of this is of 
00285	course my anger at how much my parents were like yours, but I’ve vented out a ton of that already.         
00286	    “Most of my anger right now is at your mother and father for being such bullies, and for intimidating 
00287	you into believing that ‘no matter what, it’s always your fault.’”  
00288	    I let a modicum of my anger vibrate in my voice tone: “How could they not see what a lovely person 
00289	you are!? How could they not be proud of you!? How could they pick on you like that?!” 
00290	    She looks a bit surprised, but bright-eyed. “So, Liz, let me ask you: ‘Is there any part of you that feels 
00291	like you might be justified to feel angry at them…and at those four selfish narcissists that they set you up 
00292	for?’” 
00293	    Her answer comes with some volume in less than a second. “Yeah, I feel pretty pissed right now! They 
00294	really were bullies, especially my parents! They were so much bigger than me! What could I do?” 
00295	    I say: “Nothing, then. But it’s never too late to ventilate your Just Anger about past unfairness. It’s 
00296	good practice. it builds new neural pathways to protest any new abuse in the present.”  
00297	     I then coach Liz to express her anger. She lets loose for a full minute, and then laughs in relief and 
00298	delight at the sense of empowerment it gives her. 
00299	     She sits six inches taller than I have ever seen her sit. With a big smile that tightens into a serious and 
00300	determined look, she sparks: “I finally get this reenactment thing you’ve been preaching. This last 
00301	boyfriend was not only a spitting image of my mother, he used the same kind of no-win, verbal traps to 
00302	set me up for emotional clobbering. FUCK the both of them!!!”  
00303	    After a bit of releasing laughter, she says: “I can’t believe I just said that. I can’t believe how powerful 
00304	it makes me feel. I think this is going to help me to spot narcissists before I get attached to them.”  
00305	     I respond: “I’m really inspired to see your self-compassion opening up your dormant need and right 
00306	to protect yourself. I know this may sound oxymoronic, but it was really sweet to see you getting so 
00307	healthily mad!” 
00308	    “Yeah! Thanks, Pete! I’m going to seriously think about ‘going no contact’ with my parents. There’s a 
00309	lot of people doing it on those online Recovering-from-Narcissistic-Abuse support groups that you 
00310	turned me onto.”

00312	    This vignette illustrates a process that, with much practice, can help survivors to fully care for and 
00313	champion themselves. As their self-compassion and self-protection steadily increase, so does their 
00314	ability to identify and resist reenactments. This in turn helps to resurrect an instinct to pursue truly 
00315	healthy attachments. When all this occurs, it is typically because therapy has finally met the survivor’s 
00316	crucial unmet development need for a safe and supportive relationship.  
00317	    Moreover, angering about past parental trauma can usually be done toward the internalized parents. 
00318	It does not need to be directed in person toward the actual parents to be effective, unless of course the 
00319	parents are being actively abusive in the present.     
00320	2. Cultivating an Openness to Empathy    
00321	     Compassion and empathy are what healthy mothers instinctively feel toward their children when 
00322	they are upset – when they are suffering pain, fear, shame, injustice, loneliness or depression. 
00323	Compassionate therapists can soothe the abandonment pain of survivors who still suffer the 
00324	developmental arrest of never having experienced a mother’s widely accepting kindness. A therapist can 
00325	become a survivor’s first proof that some people genuinely care about others no matter what they are 
00326	going through. 
00327	    Over the decades I have cultivated my empathy and compassion by visualizing myself in the emotional 
00328	shoes of the client. I typically do this by remembering a time when I have experienced suffering like 
00329	theirs. With decades of practice under my belt, this is now somewhat automatic. Now, when a client 
00330	discloses a painful situation, I often spontaneously come up with a matching memory with concomitant 
00331	emotional response.  
00332	    At various times of reverberating with a client, I have emotionally remembered feeling despicably 
00333	ugly, or suicidally depressed, or homicidally enraged, or racked with cancer-phobia, or desperately 
00334	lonely, or about-to evacuate-my-bowels afraid, or hopeless about making the right choice in a 
00335	monumental no-win situation, or shrunken into invisibility and pitifulness while simultaneously feeling 
00336	exposed and naked on a giant billboard.  
00337	    I can share such vulnerabilities because I have sufficiently grieved through the pain of such 
00338	experiences and come out the other side with an enhanced sense of self-acceptance. Decades ago, I was 
00339	graced by a long-term therapy with a very compassionate therapist. She helped me weather my 
00340	monsoon of shame-crippling storms to eventually dock in the port of a balanced self-acceptance. Now 
00341	balance for me resides in having healthy limits with the innate selfishness of my Id and healthy self-
00342	protection from a limit-less Superego that was once a torturous inner critic. 
00343	 
00344	    Profound distrust of other people is a defining issue in CPTSD. Since CPTSD occurs on a continuum of 
00345	severity, the effects of social anxiety range from anxious superficial relating to chronic avoidance to 
00346	severe isolation.  
00347	    Many survivors tell me that they long for social contact when they are alone, but once “connected” 
00348	soon ache to get away, and have to work hard to manage a persistent foot-out-the-door syndrome. 
00349	     Many untreated survivors find it nearly impossible to believe that anyone likes them, because they 
00350	have never had a secure attachment [the key cause of CPTSD]. Their original traumatic attachments 
00351	[trauma bonds] were rife with danger and empty of nurturance. New attachments, if any, are generally 
00352	based on repetition compulsion, and replicate the original trauma bonding. In worse case scenarios, 
00353	trauma bonds are as severe as Stockholm Syndrome. 
00354	    CPTSD makes vulnerability with others feel shameful. Opening to someone’s love and concern feels 
00355	fraught with danger. To remedy this, I model shameless-ness and self-acceptance of my own parallel 
00356	issues around distrust and fear of vulnerability. Over time this often “proves” that my compassion for 
00357	their excruciating social anxiety is real…not just a pose of kindness. This in turn helps them to trust and 
00358	benefit from my empathy and caring. With enough empathic reverberating from me, they learn that 
00359	shared pain with a safe enough person is an opportunity for comforting connection.

00423	3. Guiding the Development of Mutuality [Reciprocal Support in Relationship]  
00424	   “In any effective psychological treatment, the doctor is bound to influence the patient: but this 
00425	influence can only take place if the patient has a reciprocal influence on the doctor.” – Carl Jung goo  
00426	 
00427	    As Self-Compassion is the foundation of a healthy relationship with oneself [Self-Esteem], Mutuality -- 
00428	mutual caring and compassion -- is the essential quality of a healthy relationship with another [True 
00429	Intimacy]. Gaga  
00430	    The ultimate intersubjective process of mutuality is seen in the reciprocally soothing process that 
00431	occurs between a good enough mother and her baby. Mom’s loving gestures comfort the infant whose 
00432	baby talk utterances of pleasure comfort the Mom. On a broader level, two people with a healthy 
00433	attachment bond typically have recurring experiences of soothing verbal and emotional interchange.  
00434	    As work in the second stage matures, clients’ experiences of self-compassion [stage one] and 
00435	receiving empathy [stage two] can interweave in a yin/yang way that naturally awakens their drive 
00436	toward mutuality. This typically manifests as them benefitting from experiencing mutual empathy with 
00437	the therapist.  
00438	    I especially value the fact that vulnerable self-disclosure often shrinks the dynamic whereby clients 
00439	see me as perfect [as their parents pretended to be] and themselves as defective [as their parents saw 
00440	them]. When this shame-generating dynamic is not deconstructed, clients are often stymied by toxic 
00441	shame and feel too unsafe to disclose or even access their paramount concerns.      
00442	A Vignette about Developing Mutuality  
00443	    Rita and I have been working on her CPTSD recovery for several years. Today she starts by saying: “I’m 
00444	feeling so sad, Pete. I was rereading the part of your book about how your parents hit you in the face a 
00445	lot. And you know how much I got slapped around as a child! Suddenly, I started crying for you. I hope 
00446	it’s okay for me to feel for your little Pete. After all, how couldn’t I?...now that you’ve taught me to feel 
00447	so kindly to my inner child!” 
00448	    Early in my career, I would have deflected this or minimized it, but I can really feel her compassion, 
00449	and her pride in having developed it. She continues: “You know, Pete, I think it’s a miracle that I’m 
00450	finding so much kindness in myself. Especially, as I never once felt kindness coming from my parents!” 
00451	    I am moved to reply: “Thank you Rita, I can feel your kindness. You have such a big heart. I feel quite 
00452	comforted by it. How lucky I am to receive your empathy!” 
00453	    She simultaneously lights up and wells up in a most delightful way. She says: “I can really tell you 
00454	really mean that”, and her tears brim over her eyelids and down her cheeks.  
00455	    “You know I’m not crying now because I’m sad!” she says. 
00456	    “Yes, I think so, but tell me more about these tears!” 
00457	    “Well, they’re happy tears. I’m relieved and happy that someone finally sees how loving I really 
00458	am…but I guess I also feel sad about how rare this has been.” 
00459	    I say: “Rita, you are so indubitably a loving person. And, I’m so sorry that no one reflected that to you 
00460	as a child.” 
00461	    Rita cries out some old pain about this and settles even deeper into relief. I then comment: “I wonder, 
00462	Rita, if there’s even more in those tears…if your earlier feeling of sorrow for me around our shared 
00463	trauma, helps you get it even more deeply that I really care about you, and what you’ve been through!” 
00464	     Rita cries and laughs at the same time and says: “Well, Duh! YESSS! Of Course! And, I feel especially 
00465	empathetic to you because we both come from harsh Irish Catholic families -- those kinds that felt like 
00466	their kids needed to be smacked around a lot. No sparing the rod for us! It was like growing up in some 
00467	perverted combination of Limbo and Purgatory. We were so abandoned, so over-punished, and such 
00468	strangers to human kindness!” 
00469	    Wow, that opened up her floodgates, and moistened my eyes nicely! 
00470	    “Rita, I am so touched by you crying for our little kids, who did nothing to deserve such bullies for 
00471	parents.” Her sobs of grief and relief intermix, and after a few hiccups, relief gradually gets the upper 
00472	hand and morphs into deep and genuine laughter, in which I join her. 
00473	 
00474	     Two self-compassionate people can easily open their hearts to each other when they are in the zone 
00475	of mutuality. Such emotional and relational intelligence often creates a healing biofeedback-loop of 
00476	mutual healing. I sometimes visualize this loop as a luminous infinity symbol connecting each person’s 
00477	heart in a comforting and vitalizing way. 
00478	    Mutually attuned people coregulate and de-stress each other. They help each other move out of 
00479	excessive sympathetic nervous system arousal [the fight/flight response] into parasympathetic 
00480	relaxation. Neuroscientists are currently investigating the hypothesis that this process may be mediated 
00481	by the brain’s mirror neurons.  
00482	     
00483	    Repeated experiences of inter-compassionate connection during the mutuality stage of therapy heals 
00484	the developmental arrest of an empathy-less childhood. Ongoing reciprocal feelings of trust and safety 
00485	gradually release the survivor’s relational constrictions and create experiences of bona fide intimacy.  
00486	    In its most remedial moments, mutuality cocoons both parties in healthy attachment feelings such as 
00487	warmth, appreciation, mutual encouragement, shared tears and laughter. A recovered capacity for 
00488	mutuality eventually rewards survivors with more vital outside relationships – ones in which each 
00489	person connects with and through all their parts in ways that are equally beneficial. 
00490	    Mutuality also activates the survivor’s dormant capacity for relational efficacy – the 
00491	spiritual/emotional experience of seeing that one’s caring has a positive effect on others. Think of a 
00492	four-year-old giving a self-made Mother’s Day Card to her mother. She is thrillingly moved when her 
00493	mother wells up with tears, and gushes with authentic joy about the beauty of the gift and the sweet 
00494	lovingness of her child. Rita and I co-create a version of this experience in the vignette above. 
00495	 
00496	    Commiseration is an especially therapeutic form of mutuality. Commiseration occurs when two or 
00497	more people verbally and emotionally resonate about their upsets. Reverberating together about anger, 
00498	sadness, fear, depression or shame about childhood trauma helps survivors metabolize such feelings in a 
00499	healing way. I believe this explains why there is such a huge proliferation of online support groups for 
00500	survivors recovering from all types of trauma. 
00501	    On a larger scale, all human beings have the potential to commiserate in a way that de-shames, 
00502	soothes and co-regulates dysphoric feelings about any type of common existential suffering, e.g. 
00503	loneliness, loss, confusion and witnessing heart-breaking tragedies.  
00504	    Wonderfully, commiseration, at its best, can easily morph into a light-hearted sense of well-being, and 
00505	even shared laughter. Thirty years ago, a good friend I and were pissing and moaning at great length 
00506	about how we both failed our licensing oral exams. Suddenly, we both morphed into that sweet spot of 
00507	laughing and crying at the same time via jamming about how we’d like to “examine” our unjust 
00508	examiners – who meted out a 60% failure rate to hundreds of examinees that year. With tears running 
00509	down her face, Nancy pealed laughter as she screamed: “This is transformation-city!”.  
00510	    Now, with forty years of client work under my belt, I feel that ongoing experiences of therapeutic 
00511	mutuality were and are also healing for me. They have surely protected me from burnout, and kept my 
00512	enthusiasm for this work alive and growing.  
00513	    How tragic it is that commiseration - the most co-regulating of all intimate connections – is so rare in 
00514	Western cultures! I feel sad that so many people in industrialized societies exhibit CPTSD-like relational 
00515	arrests because of widespread taboos that stigmatize vulnerability. I see this occurring in varying degree 
00516	to many people not commonly seen as traumatized. 
00517	    Happily, I see many enclaves of people who are surmounting this cultural developmental arrest. As 
00518	alluded to above, thousands of mutually supportive online trauma-recovery groups help many people 
00519	become “good enough friends”, as well as “good enough parents”.  
00520	    Good enough parents regularly bathe their children in empathic conversational exchanges, and are 
00521	warmed by these themselves. Over time, these children grow up to form new healthy attachments as 
00522	they “pay this process forward”; as do survivors, who through relational and attachment-informed 
00523	therapy, develop the ability to form truly intimate relationships.

00553	    As powerful as mutuality can be, the focus of sessions and self-disclosure always needs to be on the 
00554	client’s concerns. Moreover, a long period of focusing singularly on the client’s psyche is often necessary 
00555	early on, and with some survivors for the duration of therapy.  
00556	    It cannot be overemphasized that clients still typically need to do a high percentage of the talking in 
00557	all stages, even during the development of the arrested ability to be dialogical. Dialogicality is an easy 
00558	conversational balance between talking and listening. Therapists enable this by keeping their self-
00559	disclosures “short-winded” -- non-monological -- and always in the service of the client. It is important to 
00560	note however, that relationships outside of therapy benefit most from a roughly 50/50 division of 
00561	listening and talking -- of focusing equally on each person’s concerns. 
00562	     
00563	    In order to model self-compassion, therapists must of course hold their own suffering with kindness. 
00564	Therapists from dysfunctional families [are there many who aren’t?] must sufficiently work through 
00565	their own family-of-origin pain before they can authentically open their psyches and hearts to similar 
00566	pain in their clients.  
00567	    Moreover, therapists who are sufficiently introspective know that they share many commonalities of 
00568	suffering with their clients. Life intrinsically brings suffering to everyone. Loneliness, loss, painful choices 
00569	and social/political injustices are existential issues that recur from time to time for us all, not just 
00570	survivors whose abject parental abandonment is an ultimate loneliness, loss and cruel blow of fate.  
00571	    Finally, those therapists who had good enough parenting may be less suitable for working with CPTSD 
00572	survivors. The stubborn, ingrained pervasiveness of the toxic critic and toxic shame may be beyond their 
00573	ken.     
00574	Guidelines for Therapeutic Self-Disclosure 
00575	    What guidelines, then, can we use to ensure that our self-disclosure is judicious and therapeutic? I 
00576	believe the following six principles help me to disclose therapeutically and steer clear of unconsciously 
00577	sharing solely for my own narcissistic gratification. 
00578	 First, I use self-disclosure sparingly. 
00579	 Second, my disclosures are offered primarily to promote a matrix of safety and trust in the 
00580	relationship. In this vein my vulnerability is offered to normalize and de-shame the deeply engrained and 
00581	multi-faceted suffering that comes from prolonged childhood trauma.  
00582	 Third, I do not share vulnerabilities that are currently raw and unintegrated. 
00583	 Fourth, I never disclose in order to work through my own “stuff,” or to meet my own narcissistic 
00584	need for verbal ventilation or personal edification. 
00585	 Fifth, while I may share my appreciation or be touched by a client’s attempt or offer to focus on 
00586	my vulnerabilities, I never accept the offer. I gently thank them for their concern, remind them that our 
00587	work is client-centered, and let them know that I have an outside support network. 
00588	              Sixth, it is important to emphasize that I only self-disclose in response to cues from the client. I 
00589	must sense that they are developmentally ready for and open to this type of help.
