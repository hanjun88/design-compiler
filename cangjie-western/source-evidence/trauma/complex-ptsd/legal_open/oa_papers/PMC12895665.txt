00001	Background
00002	Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) among survivors of Sexual and Gender-Based Violence (SGBV) in war-affected zones is a pressing public health issue, as these individuals often endure compounded trauma from both the violence they experience and the broader context of conflict. Post-traumatic stress disorders are overlooked in Ethiopian society, despite the country having the greatest number of victims of violence and its detrimental effects on mental health. This study aimed to explore Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder among Sexual and Gender-Based Violence Survivors attending one-stop centers in war-affected Zones, in Ethiopia.
00003	Methods
00004	A cross-sectional study design was carried out with 321 individuals at least 15 years old at the time of attendance at the one-stop center in December/2022 and January 2023. The research participants were chosen by a systematic random sampling method. The outcome measure used in the study was the Post-traumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL). Data was entered into the Epi-data version 4.6 and it was exported to SPSS version 26 statistical software for further analysis. Pretested, structured questionnaires and face-to-face interviews were used for data collection. To identify factors associated with PTSD, bivariate and multivariable logistic regressions were performed. Variables in multivariable logistic regression with a p-value of less than 0.05 were considered significant, and the strength of the association was present using adjusted OR (AOR) with a 95% confidence interval.
00005	Results
00006	The prevalence of posttraumatic stress disorder among participants was 36.4% (95% CI:30.8,41.7). In multivariable analysis, experiencing multiple violence history (AOR = 3.34; 95% CI 1.44, 7.77), a family history of suicide (AOR = 3.26; 95% CI 1.49, 7.16), and having current depression (AOR = 8.71; 95% CI 4.84,15.67) were variables significantly associated with PTSD.
00007	Conclusion
00008	This study showed PTSD is extremely common among SGBV survivors in war-affected regions, with experiencing multiple violence, a family history of suicide, and depression as major identified contributing factors. Based on this finding, urgent mental health services and intrauma informed care are needed for the survivor. However, increased investment is needed to expand access and improve the quality of these services. Further research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of tailored interventions in improving outcomes in this population.
00009	Keywords
00010	Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a debilitating condition that emerges after exposure to a traumatic event and is characterized by four hallmark clusters of symptoms: re-experiencing, avoidance, negative cognitions and mood, and hyperarousal [1]. PTSD frequently starts in childhood and is associated with both natural and man-made disasters, including child abuse, sexual assault, protracted warfare, and torture [2].
00011	Globally, at least 89.3 million people have been displaced due to numerous factors such as armed conflicts, civil war, violence, human rights violations, persecution, and discrimination [3]. According to the United Nations Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) Global Trends report (2021), over 26 million refugee women and girls are disproportionately impacted by emergencies and face various hazards related to their Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH) risk worldwide. As a result, they all require services related to SRH [4].
00012	Sexual and Gender-Based Violence (SGBV) is more likely to occur among women and girls in conflict-affected settings [5]. World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 35% of women globally experience SGBV at some point in their lives. In African, women living in conflict-ridden areas are at an even higher risk [6], with violence against women reported at 71% in Ethiopia [7].
00013	In 2021, civil conflict in Northern Ethiopia between the Ethiopian National Defence Forces (ENDF) and The Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF) resulted in thousands of deaths, injuries, and displaced nearly 2 million people [8]. SGBV is a serious public health issue with lasting consequences and by definition, a violation of human rights, it has a detrimental effect on women’s and girls’ SRH and general welfare. As a result, when SGBV survivors receive inadequate or delayed care, it can have serious long-term effects [9, 10]. Rooted in discriminatory social norms and male-dominated power structures, SGBV persists due to impunity, fear of consequences, and social stigma, perpetuating violence and underreporting [11].
00014	Among the negative consequences of SGBV are PTSD, self-stigma, and discrimination [12]. The National comorbidity survey found that 10.4% of women had PTSD at some point in their lives [13]. If this PTSD is left untreated, it has been associated with decreased physical recovery, social functioning, and quality of life [14]. Therefore, the detrimental effects of SGBV must be addressed in order to save lives and enhance the well-being of those impacted by crises; however, survivors frequently choose not to disclose or report such instances, nor do they seek out or seek assistance [15]. One-stop centers have been established to provide integrated support services, including medical care, legal assistance, and psychological counseling, aimed at facilitating recovery for survivors. However, barriers such as stigma, shame, limited awareness of available services, and inadequate privacy within these centers hinder access to essential care [16].
00015	There is a wide variation in PTSD prevalence across countries. This difference may show the type, intensity and duration of traumatic stressors experienced by women as well as quality od social support. Research has shown that the prevalence of PTSD among SGBV survivors, including Nigeria (63%) [17], Colombia (88%) [18] and Palestine (68.9%) [19], Uganda, (11.8%) [20], South Africa (21.0%) [21], and Sri Lanka (2.3%). For example the higher prevalence of PTSD in Colombia and Palestine may be related to prolonged conflict and limited social support, whereas lower rates in Sri Lanka could be could show variation in study populations, timing after trauma exposure, or stronger social support networks.
00016	Many factors contribute to the development of PTSD in SGBV survivors after exposure to traumatic events. Different types of violence (psychological, sexual, and economic violence), anxiety, and depression are all linked to greater rates of PTSD [22]. Additionally, being younger, single and internally displaced, as well as not engaging in unpaid labor such as childcare, should be positively associated with experiencing sexual violence in conflict affected areas [23]. Significant mental health effects from SGBV included psychological distress, depression, suicide thoughts, and PTSD [24].
00017	Despite the high risk of PTSD among SGBV survivors in post-conflict settings, there is no research has been conducted on one-stop centers in the war-affected area of Northeast Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by determining the prevalence of PTSD and identifying its associated factors among SGBV suvivors attending one-stop centers in war-affected Zones in Ethiopia.
00018	Method and materials
00019	Study design, period, and area
00020	A cross-sectional study was conducted in war-affected South Wollo, North Wollo, and Waghimra Zones of the Amhara region between December 1/2022 and January 30/2023. Based on the 2007 Census conducted by the Central Statistical Agency of Ethiopia (CSA), the zones have a total population of 2,518,862, 1,500,303, and 41,8817, respectively. Dessie, Woldia, and Sekota are the capital cities of South Wollo, North Wollo, and Waghimra Zones, with distances from Addis Ababa of 400 km, 503 km, and 720 km respectively [25]. Zones have one-stop centers in their capitals, administered by Dessie Referral Hospital, Woldia Specialized Hospital, and Sekota Tefera Hailu Memorial General Hospital. One-stop centers are equipped with multidisciplinary professionals, including social workers, counselors, general practitioners, nurses, and psychiatry professionals. According to the Amhara Public Health Institute (APHI), at the beginning of the study period, found that 700, 200, and 300 survivors of SGBV were attending one-stop centers in the zones. Dessie, a well-equipped one-stop center, was established before the conflict began, while Woldia and Sekota were not. Different NGOs were deployed as professionals in these centers during the post-war period.
00021	Population
00022	The target population comprised all SGBV survivors aged 15 years and above attending one-stop centers. The study population included all SGBV suvivors aged 15 years and above attending one-stop centers in the selected areas during the data collection period.
00023	Inclusion and exclusion criteria
00024	Participants were survivors of SGBV. Participants were at least 15 years old at the time of attendance at the one-stop center. All study participants were female, and met the inclusion criteria. Survivors, who were unable to communicate effectively due to language barriers or cognitive impairments as well as individuals experiencing severe acute psychotic episodes were excluded from the study.

00046	Independent variables
00047	Sociodemographic variables (age, marital status, employment status, education level, and living arrangements), psychosocial factors (social support), clinical factors (prior mental health conditions, history of mental health conditions in the family, family history of suicide, history of chronic illness, depression, and violence factors (number of perpetrators and number of violent acts) were the independent variables in this study.
00048	Operational definitions
00049	Sexual and gender based violence survivors (SGBV)
00050	Individuals identified by the clinician as survivors of non-consensual sexual contact who were unable to consent or refuse [5].
00051	One-stop center (OSC)
00052	A facility that provide integrated, multi-disciplinary services for SGBV victims in a single physical location [29].
00053	Post-traumatic stress disorder
00054	Scoring ≥ 44 on the (PCL-C) [26].
00055	Depression
00056	Scoring ≥ 10 on the PHQ-9 [27].
00057	Poor social support
00058	Using Oslo Social Support Scale (OSSS-3), a score from 3 to 8 was considered as poor social support, 9–11 moderate social support, and 12–14 as strong social support [28].
00059	Data collection method and tool
00060	The first part of the questionnaire was designed to collect respondents’ socio-demographic, trauma, clinical, and social characteristics which was collected by face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire. To assess the level of PTSD among SGBV survivors the Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist (PCL) was used. The Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist (PCL) is an easily administered self-report rating scale for assessing DSM-V symptoms of PTSD. The PCL-C consists of 17 items and the responses ranged from “Not at all [1] to extremely [5]”. The possible scores range from 17 to 85. Based on the severe nature of GBV as well as recommendations from previous works on the population, scores ≥ 44 on a scale were taken as a cut-off point for the current study. It is a well-studied tool for the measurement of PTSD among different samples. It has excellent internal consistency (0.97) and test re-test reliability (0.96) [26]. The internal consistency (Cronbach alpha) of (PCL) in this study was 0.87. The PCL-5’s validity and reliability have been examined and demonstrated in several nations, including Iraq (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85) [29], and Zimbabwe (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.92) [30].
00061	To assess the level of depression among SGBV victims; we used the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). It has nine items ranging from 0 to 27. In the response categories of PHQ-9, each item is responded from 0 (not at all), 1(several days), 2 (more than half the days), and 3 (nearly every day). A total score of 10 or higher indicates depression [31]. Previously, the screening tool was validated in the Ethiopian population with a sensitivity of 86% and specificity of 67% [32]. In several nations, the PHQ-9 validity and reliability have been evaluated and verified for usage in community studies [27]. The internal consistency (Cronbach alpha) of (PHQ-9) in this study was 0.81. Social support is measured by using the 3-item Oslo Social Support Scale (OSSS-3). The total score has a range of 3 to 14, where high scores indicate strong social support and low scores indicate poor social support. A score from 3 to 8 was considered poor social support, 9–11 as moderate social support, and 12–14 as strong social support [28]. Socio-demographic factors, the presence of history of mental health conditions, family mental health conditions and suicide, chronic diseases such as hypertension, trauma-related factors were used on “yes/no“ response questionnaires and were operationalized according to different works of literature.
00062	Data quality management
00063	The English version questionnaire was translated into Amharic (the local language) and back-translated back to English by an independent translator to check for consistency and understandability of the questionnaire. We used standard tools to measure the outcome variables. Data collectors and supervisors received two days of training on ethics, privacy, and confidentiality, and were oriented to provide first-line support when needed. Before the actual data collection a pretest was conducted with 5% of participants (n = 16) at Tefera Hailu Memorial General Hospital, Sekota. The pretest aimed to check the reliability and validity of the prepared questionnaire, to evaluate the clarity of the questions, the time needed to complete it, knowing the respondents’ comfort with being assessed, and gather feedback for improvement. Supervision by a psychologist and principal investigator was held regularly during the data collection period and the collected data was checked daily for completeness and consistency. Finally, all the questionnaires were collected back from the data collectors, and summarized for data interpretation.
00064	Data processing and analysis
00065	After the data were checked and cleaned, it was entered into the Epi-data version 4.6 and it was exported to SPSS version 26 statistical software for further analysis. Independent variables with a p-value of < 0.25 in the bi-variable model were included in the multivariable regression model to control for possible confounding effects. All variables with a p-value of < 0.05 in the multivariable model were considered statistically significant and the strength of the association was presented by an odds ratio of 95% C.I. Multicollinearity was checked by Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) (maximum VIF = 2.8). No issues of multicollinearity were detected, and overall model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, which indicated a good fit with a p-value greater than 0.05. Then the final result was presented in the form of text, a table, or a graph. Tests of multicollinearity or the distinct impact of predictors on outcome variables were conducted by looking at tolerance and variance inflation factors.

00078	Prevalence of posttraumatic stress disorder among participants
00079	This study showed the overall prevalence of posttraumatic stress disorder among SGBV survivors attending one-stop centers in war-affected areas was 36.4% [(95% CI:30.8,41.7)].
00080	Factors associated with PTSD among sexual and gender-based violence survivors attending one-stop centers in war-affected zones of Ethiopia
00081	Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analysis was done to identify factors associated with PTSD among SGBV Survivors. In the bivariate analysis, marital status, occupational status, living arrangement, number of perpetrators, number of violence experienced, social support, family suicidal history, family history of mental health conditions s, and depression showed a p-value of < 0.25 and became candidates for multivariable analysis. In multivariable binary logistic regression, variables; number of violence experienced, family suicidal history, and depression were found to be statistically associated with PTSD at a p-value less than 0.05.
00082	Those participants who had experienced two or more violence history were 3.34 times more likely to develop PTSD as compared with participants who experienced one violence history [AOR = 3.34; 95% CI (1.44, 7.77)]. Similarly, those respondents who had a family history of suicide were 3.26 times more likely to have PTSD as compared with participants who did not have a family history of suicide [AOR = 3.26; 95% CI (1.49, 7.16)]. Furthermore, the odds of having PTSD among SGBV survivors who had depression were about 8.71 times higher as compared with the referent groups [AOR = 8.71; 95% CI (4.84,15.67)] (Table 4).

00094	Discussion
00095	The current study set out to estimate the prevalence of PTSD and associated factors among SGBV victims attending one-stop centers in war-affected Zones in Ethiopia. Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) is a severe psychological condition resulting from significant trauma, affecting individuals’ mental health and physical well-being. It is particularly prevalent among victims of violence, with over 50% developing it.
00096	Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) is linked to cardiovascular disease and diabetes, and its prevalence is higher among rape victims. It’s societal costs are significant, resulting in increased healthcare utilization, lost productivity, and financial burdens. Diagnosing PTSD can help reduce stigma, improve quality of life, and promote recovery [33]. The result of the current study showed the overall prevalence of PTSD was 36.4%. Experienced two or more violence history, family history of suicide, as well as current depression were significantly associated with PTSD.
00097	The PTSD prevalence among SGBV victims (36.4%) aligns with findings from a study of Ethiopian landslide survivors, which reported 37.3% [34]. This study, however, paled in comparison to research conducted on victims in Nigeria (63%) [17], Colombia (88%) [18] and Palestine (68.9%) [19]. The participants’ demographic composition may account for the discrepancy. For example, the Palestinian study was limited to youth who had severe injuries as a result of ongoing hostilities and were residing in camps for refugees during the Al-Aqsa intifada. The young people were especially vulnerable because of the acute and long-lasting stress. As a result, several researches have shown that the frequency of PTSD rises in direct proportion to the degree of exposure to traumatic experiences, such as the number or intensity of the events experienced [27]. The use of different PTSD measurement instruments and cut-off points, exposure to multiple traumas, study design, and the kind and extent of the accidents revealed in the study are additional possible reasons for this disparity.
00098	This figure was more than that of studies conducted in Uganda (11.8%) [20], South Africa (21.0%) [21], Sri Lanka (2.3%) [35], and Sindh (9%) [36]. The possible variation might have resulted in young women living in informal settlements being included in South Africa, and posttraumatic stress disorder was assessed seven years after the conflict in Uganda [20]. Furthermore, this variation may be due to differences in the instruments used; in Uganda, the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) [20], in Sri Lanka the Composite International Diagnostic Interview (CIDI) [35], and in Sindh DSM-IV- TR was used [36], and in this study, the PCL-C was used, along with Extended Standards Criteria A that was modified with a better internal consistent to measure PTSD [37]. A possible explanation could also be the length of time that the subjects were exposed to traumatic events; the study was done seven years after the conflict in Uganda and after twenty years of forced displacement in Sri Lanka; whereas the current study was carried out ten months after trauma exposure. Therefore, the longer exposure to the traumatic incident was more likely to cause a recall bias-induced reduction in magnitude. Additionally, the diversity in the results might be explained by the various types of trauma exposure, sampling procedures, and sociocultural factors.
00099	Regarding the associated factors, those respondents who had experienced two or more violence history were 3.34 times more likely to have PTSD as compared to participants with a single violent history. This finding was in agreement with different studies in Nepal reported that women were at higher risk due to the experience of domestic violence and other stressful events related to their intimate partners [38]. Research shows that individuals who have experienced multiple traumatic events, particularly those involving assaultive violence, exhibit a markedly higher risk of PTSD [39].
00100	In this study, participants with a family history of suicide were 3.26 times more likely to develop PTSD than those without such a history. Previous research supports this association, suggesting that both genetic predispositions and shared environmental factors contribute to increase the risk of PTSD. Furthermore, frequent exposure to family suicidal behaviors may increase traumatic response and psychological distress, thereby increase susceptibility to PTSD [40].
00101	Furthermore, the odds of having PTSD among SGBV survivors who had current depression were about 8.71 times higher as compared with the referent groups. This is consistent with research conducted in Kenya [41]. This could be because respondents without symptoms of depression are less likely than participants with depressive symptoms to report having gone through stressful experiences, which increases the likelihood of developing PTSD [18]. Past experiences with depression and other psychological issues are another factor that increases the risk of developing PTSD [35].
00102	Limitations
00103	The study on Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) among SGBV survivors attending one-stop centers in war-affected zones has some limitations that might be considered. Firstly, the cross-sectional design of the study restricts the ability to establish causal relationships between SGBV experiences and PTSD, limiting the interpretation of the findings. Secondly, recall bias and social desirability may have influenced participants’ responses, leading to inaccuracies in self-reported data. Despite, these limitations, the study has notable strengths. It adresesses a serious public health problem in a highly vulnerable population and the relatively large sample size given contextual challenges of data collection enhances the strength of this study.
00104	This study showed a high prevalence of posttraumatic stress disorder among SGBV victims attending one-stop centers in war-affected areas in North East Ethiopia. Experiences of multiple violence, family history of suicide, and current depression were found to be significant predictors of PTSD. The implications of studying PTSD among SGBV survivors in war-affected areas are profound, calling for an integrated approach to health care that addresses the complex needs of these individuals. By prioritizing mental health support and tailored interventions, service providers can significantly improve the outcomes for survivors of SGBV. At the same time, a broader strategy are needed to prevent the use of sexual violence as a weapon of war and to strengthen accountability systems to bring perpetrators to justice. In light of the mental health impact demonstrated by this study, the integration of mental health care with legal and social protection may be a valuable approach for policymakers to consider in supporting survivors and addressing their complex needs.
00105	Implications
00106	PTSD among sexual gender-based violence (SGBV) victims at one-stop centers in war-affected areas has significant implications for enhancing mental health support. It provides the design of targeted interventions and inform train programs for health workers to recognize and address these issues effectively. The result of these findings can inform the policy development, proper resource allocation, and provide special supports for the victims. Additionally, raising community awareness and reducing stigma may improve social support and encourage help-seeking behavior, ultimately contributing to better mental health outcomes for this vulnerable population.
