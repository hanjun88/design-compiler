00002	East Bay Therapist ,  Sept 2008 
00003	By Pete Walker

00006	 
00007	More and more traumatology pundits see attachment disorder as one of the key 
00008	symptoms of Complex PTSD. In the psycoeducational phases of working with 
00009	traumatized clients [outlined in my article: “Managing Emotional Flashbacks in Complex 
00010	PTSD”], I typically describe attachment disorder as the result of growing up with primary 
00011	caretakers who were regularly experienced as dangerous – dangerous by contemptuous 
00012	voice or heavy hand, or more insidiously, dangerous by remoteness and indifference. 
00013	Recurring abuse and neglect habituate children to living in fear and sympathetic nervous 
00014	system arousal, which over time creates in them an easily triggerable abandonment 
00015	melange of overwhelming fear, shame and depression. 
00016	 
00017	 A child with parents, who are unable or unwilling to provide safe enough attachment, 
00018	has no one to whom she can bring her whole developing self. No one is there for 
00019	reflection, validation and guidance. No one is safe enough to go to for comfort or help in 
00020	times of trouble. There is no one to cry to, to protest unfairness to, to seek commiseration 
00021	from for hurts, mistakes, accidents, and betrayals. No one is safe enough to shine with, to 
00022	do “show and tell” with, to be reflected as a subject of pride…to even practice the all-
00023	important intimacy-building skills of conversation. In the paraphrased words of more 
00024	than one of my clients: “Talking to Mom was like giving ammunition to the enemy. 
00025	Anything I said could and would be used against me. People always tell me that I don’t 
00026	seem to have much to say for myself.” 
00027	 
00028	Those with Complex PTSD-spawned attachment disorders never learn the 
00029	communication skills that engender closeness and a sense of belonging. When it comes to 
00030	relating, they are typically plagued by debilitating social anxiety, and social phobia when 
00031	they are at the severe end of the continuum of PTSD. Many of the clients who come 
00032	through my door have never had a safe enough relationship. Repetition compulsion has 
00033	compelled them to unconsciously seek out relationships in adulthood that traumatically 
00034	reenact the abusive and/or abandoning dynamics of their childhood caretakers. For many 
00035	such clients, we are their first legitimate shot at a safe and nurturing relationship; and if 
00036	we are not skilled enough to create the degree of safety they need to begin the long 
00037	journey towards developing good enough trust, we may be their last. 
00038	 
00039	As the importance of this understanding ripens in me, I increasingly embrace an 
00040	Intersubjective or Relational approach. I believe that it is the quality [rather qualities] of 
00041	the clients’ relationship with me that can provide a corrective emotional experience that 
00042	saves them from being doomed to a lifetime of superficial connection, or worse, social 
00043	isolation and alienation. Moreover, I notice that without the development of a modicum 
00044	of trust with me, many of my PTSD clients are seriously delimited in their receptivity to 
00045	my guidance, as well as to the ameliorative effects of my empathy. In this regard then, I 
00046	will describe four key qualities of relating that I believe are essential to the development 
00047	of trust and subsequent relational healing. These are Empathy, Authentic Vulnerability, 
00048	Dialogicality and Collaborative Relationship Repair. 
00049	 
00050	1. Empathy. I used to assume that the merits of empathy were a given, but I have  
00051	sadly heard too many stories of empathy-impoverished therapy. In this regard, I will 
00052	simply say here that if we are hard and unsympathetic with our clients, we trigger the 
00053	same sense of danger and abandonment in them that they experienced with their parents.   
00054	In terms of a definition, I especially like Kohut’s statement that: “Empathy involves 
00055	immersing oneself in another’s psychological state by feeling one’s self into the other’s 
00056	experience.” When I delve deeply enough into a client’s experience, no matter how 
00057	initially off-putting or bizarre, I inevitably find psychological sense in it, especially when 
00058	I grok its flashback components.  
00059	 
00060	Empathy, of course, deepens via careful listening and full elicitation of the client’s 
00061	experience, along with the time-honored techniques of mirroring and paraphrasing which 
00062	show the client the degree to which we get her. Noticing, and when appropriate sharing 
00063	my subjective free associations – especially when they are my own emotionally 
00064	analogous and autobiographical reverberations - often enhances my empathic attunement 
00065	and ability to reflect back to her in an emotionally accurate and validating way. 
00066	 
00067	2. Authentic Vulnerability. The process of emotional reflection is part of a second 
00068	quality of intimate relating: authentic vulnerability. I have found emotional reflection to 
00069	be irreplaceable in fostering the development of trust and real relational intimacy. I came 
00070	to value this the hard way via its absence in my first personal experiences with therapists 
00071	who were of the old, “blank screen” school – who were distant, laconic and over-
00072	withholding in their commitment to “optimal frustration.” Therapy was actually counter-
00073	therapeutic and shame exacerbating for me as we co-created and reenacted a defective 
00074	child/perfect parent dynamic. Gratefully, I eventually realized that I had unresolved 
00075	attachment issues, and sought out a Relational therapist who valued the use of her own 
00076	vulnerable and emotionally authentic self as a tool in therapy. Her temperate and timely 
00077	self-disclosures - “God, the holidays can be awful;”  “Sorry I missed what you said, I got 
00078	a little distracted with anxiety about my dental appointment this afternoon;” “I feel sad 
00079	that you’re mother was so cold;” “It makes me angry that you were so bullied;” - helped 
00080	me to deconstruct a veneer of invincibility I had built as a child to hide my pain. Her 
00081	modeling that anger, sadness, fear, and depression were emotions that could be healthily 
00082	felt and expressed rather than buried in shame and “gotten over” helped me to renounce 
00083	the pain-repressing, emotional perfectionism that I had adopted in the hope of being 
00084	loved. I needed this kind of modeling, as so many of my clients have, to begin to emerge 
00085	from my fear of being attacked, shamed or abandoned for having dysphoric feelings. In 
00086	order to let go of my Sisyphean salvation fantasy of achieving constant happiness, I 
00087	needed to experience that all the less than shiny bits of me were acceptable to another 
00088	human being. Seeing that she was comfortable with and accepting of her own unhappy 
00089	feelings eventually convinced me that she really was not disgusted by mine. 
00090	 
00091	The therapist’s judicious use of vulnerable self-disclosure helps the client move out of the 
00092	shame-lined slippery pit of emotional perfectionism. Here are some examples that I use: 
00093	“I feel really sad about what happened to you;" " I feel really angry that you got stuck 
00094	with such a god-awful family;" "When I'm temporarily confused and don't know what to 
00095	say or do, I…;" “When I’m having a shame attack or feel suddenly triggered into fear, 
00096	I…” "When my inner critic is overreacting, I remind myself of the Winnicottian concept 
00097	that I only have to be a ‘good enough person;’” My most consistent feedback from past 
00098	clients is that responses like these - especially ones that normalize fear and depression - 
00099	helped them immeasurably to deconstruct their perfectionism, and open up to self 
00100	compassion and self acceptance. 
00101	 
00102	What guidelines, then, can we use to insure that our self-disclosure is judicious and 
00103	therapeutic? I believe the following five principles help me to disclose therapeutically 
00104	and steer clear of unconsciously sharing for my own narcissistic gratification. First, I use 
00105	self-disclosure sparingly. Second, my disclosures are offered primarily to promote a 
00106	matrix of safety and trust in the relationship. In this vein my vulnerability is offered to 
00107	normalize and de-shame the inexorable, existential imperfection of the human condition, 
00108	i.e., we all make mistakes, suffer painful feelings, experience confusions, etc. Third, I do 
00109	not share vulnerabilities that are currently raw and unintegrated. Fourth, I never disclose 
00110	in order to work through my own “stuff,” or to meet my own narcissistic need for verbal 
00111	ventilation or edification. Fifth, while I may share my appreciation or be touched by a 
00112	client's attempt or offer to focus on or soothe my vulnerabilities, I never accept the offer; 
00113	I gently remind them that our work is client-centered, and that I have an outside support 
00114	network.  
00115	 
00116	Emotional self-disclosure and the sharing of parallel trauma history. Since many of 
00117	my clients have sought my services after reading my autobiographical book on recovery 
00118	from the dysfunctional family, self-disclosure about my past trauma is sometimes a moot 
00119	point. This condition has at the same time helped me realize how powerful this kind of 
00120	disclosure can be in healing shame and cultivating hope. Over and over clients have told 
00121	me that my vulnerable pragmatic stories of working through the traumatizing effects of 
00122	my parents varied abuse and neglect has given them courage to engage this long difficult 
00123	journey. Now whether or not someone has read my book, I will – with appropriate clients 
00124	- judiciously and sparingly share my own experiences of dealing with an issue they have 
00125	currently brought up. I do this both to psychoeducate them and to model ways that they 
00126	might address their own analogous concerns. One common example sounds like this: “I 
00127	hate flashbacks too. Even though I get them much less than when I started this work, 
00128	falling back into that old fear and shame is so awful.” Occasionally, I match them and let 
00129	myself tear up with authentic commiseration while relaying this experience to them. Here 
00130	is another example: “I really reverberate with your feelings of hopelessness and 
00131	powerlessness around the inner critic. In the early stages of this work, I often felt 
00132	overwhelmingly frustrated. It seemed that trying to shrink it actually made it worse. But 
00133	now tens of thousands of repetitions of thought-stopping and thought-substitution have 
00134	very gradually reduced it to a mere shadow of its former self.” [See “Shrinking The Inner 
00135	Critic in Complex PTSD”.] 
00136	 
00137	3. Dialogicality. Dialogicality occurs when two people move fluidly and interchangeably 
00138	between speaking [an aspect of healthy narcissism] and listening [an aspect of healthy 
00139	codependence.] Such reciprocal interactions energize both participants. Dialogical 
00140	relating stands in contrast to the monological energy-theft that characterizes interactions 
00141	whereby a narcissist pathologically exploits a codependent’s listening defense. 
00142	In therapy, dialogicality develops out of a teamwork approach - a mutual brainstorming 
00143	about issues and concerns that cultivates full exploration of ambivalences, conflicts and 
00144	other life difficulties. Dialogicality is enhanced when the therapist offers feedback with a 
00145	take-it-or-leave-it intent. Dialogicality also implies respectful mutuality. It stands in stark 
00146	contrast to the blank screen neutrality and abstinence of traditional psychodynamic 
00147	therapy, which all too often reenacts the verbal and emotional neglect of childhood - 
00148	flashing the client back into feelings of abandonment, and triggering them to retreat into 
00149	“safe”, superficial disclosure, ever-growing muteness and/or early flight from therapy. 
00150	All this being said, extensive dialogicality is often inappropriate in the early stages of 
00151	therapy especially when the client’s normal narcissistic needs have never been gratified, 
00152	and remain developmentally arrested. In such cases, clients need to be extensively heard. 
00153	They need to discover through the agency of spontaneous self-expression the nature of 
00154	their own feelings, needs, preferences and views. This is the work of giving the as yet, 
00155	unformed ego room to grow and break free from the hegemony of the traumatically 
00156	inculcated superego [See again: “Shrinking The Inner Critic in Complex PTSD”.]  
00157	This does not mean however, that the client benefits when the therapist retreats into 
00158	extremely polarized listening. Most benefit, as early as the first session, from hearing 
00159	something real or “personal” from the therapist. This helps overcome the shame-inducing 
00160	potential inherent in the “One-seen [client] / Unseen [therapist]” dynamic. It is interesting 
00161	to note in this regard that CAMFT surveyed California therapists about fifteen years ago 
00162	concerning their therapy preferences, and upwards of ninety percent emphasized that they 
00163	did not want a blank screen therapist, but rather one who occasionally offered opinions 
00164	and advice. For twenty years, I have been routinely asking clients in the first session: 
00165	“Have your previous experiences in therapy given you any sense of what you want… and 
00166	what you don’t want to happen here with me?” How frequently they have responded 
00167	similarly to the therapists in the survey. Experience has taught me that such clients 
00168	typically benefit markedly from early psychoeducation about Complex PTSD. 
00169	 
00170	I sometimes wonder whether the rise in popularity of Coaching has been a reaction to the 
00171	various traditional forms of therapeutic neglect – none worse than when the therapist fails 
00172	to notice or challenge a client’s incessant, self-hating diatribes. This, I believe, is akin to 
00173	tacitly approving of and silently colluding with the inner critic. Perhaps therapeutic 
00174	withholding and abstinence derives from the absent father syndrome that afflicts so many 
00175	American families. Perhaps traditional psychotherapy overemphasizes the mothering 
00176	principles of listening and unconditional love, and neglects the fathering principles of 
00177	encouragement and guidance that coaching specializes in. 
00178	 
00179	Too much coaching is, of course, as counter-therapeutic and unbalanced as too much 
00180	listening. Just as it takes fathering and mothering to raise a balanced child, mothering and 
00181	fathering principles are needed to meet the developmental arrests of the attachment-
00182	deprived client. The sophisticated therapist values both and intuitively oscillates between 
00183	the two, depending on the developmental needs of the client in the moment – sometimes 
00184	guiding with psychoeducation and active positive noticing, and sometimes receptively 
00185	nurturing the client’s evolving practice of her own spontaneously arising self-expression. 
00186	Finally, I often notice that the last phase of therapy is characterized by a true, increasing 
00187	dialogicality – a balanced fluidity of talking and listening. This conversational reciprocity 
00188	is a key characteristic of healthy intimacy, and when therapy is successful, progress in 
00189	mutuality has already begun to serve the client in creating healthier relationships in the 
00190	outside world.

00192	Dialogicality and the Four F’s. [The Four F’s refers to four different defensive styles in 
00193	Complex PTSD: Fight, Flight, Freeze, and Fawn, which correspond respectively with 
00194	Narcissistic, Obsessive-Compulsive, Dissociative, and Codependent defensive styles (see 
00195	“The Four F’s: A Trauma Typology in Complex PTSD”.)]   
00196	 
00197	While childhood abandonment, compounded by repetition compulsion in later 
00198	relationships, leaves all untherapized Four F types dieing - or rather starving - to be 
00199	heard, their dialogical needs in therapy vary considerably over the course of therapy.  
00200	The fawn or codependent type, who discovered ersatz and pathological attachment in 
00201	childhood by becoming a parent’s sounding board or shoulder to cry on, may use his 
00202	listening defense to prematurely elicit dialogicality, and may even invoke the careless 
00203	therapist into narcissistically monologing herself. This type and the freeze type, who 
00204	learned early to seek safety in the camouflage of silence, often have to be persistently 
00205	encouraged to discover and talk about their own inner experience to discover that their 
00206	healthy narcissistic needs can be met in the process of relating. It is also important to note 
00207	here that freeze types – as they struggle to learn to talk about themselves - can easily get 
00208	lost in seemingly relevant free associations, that on closer observation turn out to be 
00209	flights of fantasy and dream elaboration that are merely manifestations of their 
00210	dissociative defense – unconsciously constructed to keep them buoyant above their 
00211	undealt with emotional pain. 
00212	 
00213	On the other hand, the fight or narcissistic type - often already habituated to holding court 
00214	in a desperate attempt to attain connection – may have her talking-defense exacerbated by 
00215	months of uninterrupted listening. I remember one man early in my career whose barely 
00216	extant ability to listen reciprocally to his wife virtually disappeared, as my fifty minutes 
00217	of uninterrupted listening became his new norm and expectation in relationship. A 
00218	therapist, who is a fawn type herself, may be prone to countertransferrentially 
00219	disappearing into a listening and eliciting defense to avoid the difficult, and sometimes 
00220	scary work of gradually insinuating herself into the relationship and nudging it towards 
00221	dialogicality.  
00222	 
00223	Finally flight or obsessive-compulsive types sometimes look more dialogical than other 
00224	types, but if we do not steer them into their deeper, emotionally based concerns, they may 
00225	remain stuck and floundering in obsessive perseverations about superficial worries that 
00226	are little more than left brain dissociations from repressed pain. 
00227	All types of course use left or right brain dissociative process to avoid feeling and 
00228	grieving their childhood losses. Dialogicality then can be oriented toward helping them to 
00229	uncover and verbally and emotionally vent their ungrieved hurts. 
00230	 
00231	4. Collaborative Rapport Repair is the capacity to not only survive but also grow closer 
00232	from the inevitable breaks in attunement that are existential to every relationship of 
00233	substance. Rapport repair is probably the most transformative, intimacy-building process 
00234	that a therapist can model. I guide this process from a perspective that recognizes that 
00235	there is almost always a mutual contribution to any misattunement or conflict. 
00236	Consequently, a mutually respectful dialogical process is typically needed to repair 
00237	rapport. I often initiate the repair process with two contiguous interventions. First I 
00238	identify the misattunement  [e.g., “I think I might have misunderstood you”], and then I 
00239	model vulnerability by describing what might be my contribution to the disconnection. 
00240	Abbreviated examples of this are: “I think I may have just been somewhat preachy…or 
00241	tired…or inattentive…or impatient…or tr iggered by my own transference.” Owning 
00242	one’s part in a conflict validates the normality of relational disappointment and the art of 
00243	amiable disagreement. It also helps to deconstruct the client’s tendency to project his own 
00244	need for perfection onto the therapist, while at the same time modeling a constructive 
00245	approach to conflicts outside the therapeutic relationship. I believe one of the most 
00246	common reasons clients terminate prematurely is the gradual accumulation of 
00247	dissatisfactions that they do not feel safe enough to bring up or talk about. How sad it is 
00248	that all kinds of promising relationships wither and die for want of the ability to safely 
00249	work through differences and conflict.

00251	Earned Secure Attachment is a newly recognized category of healthy attachment. Many 
00252	attachment therapists believe that efficacious treatment can help a Complex PTSD client 
00253	“earn” at least one truly intimate relationship - a good enough and intimacy-rich 
00254	attachment. I sometimes tell clients they are intimacy-rich to the degree that they can 
00255	connect with another and communicate authentically from all of their experiences: 
00256	success or failure; happiness or depression; love or alienation; trust or fear; peace or 
00257	anger, etc. I believe the principles outlined in this article are keys to achieving earned 
00258	secure attachment, and that the intimacy-modeling relationship with the therapist can 
00259	become a transitional relationship that leads to the attainment of at least one good enough 
00260	relationship outside the therapy. I have repeatedly seen this result with my most 
00261	successful clients, and I am grateful to report that I have experienced this myself through 
00262	the agency of my own personal therapy.
