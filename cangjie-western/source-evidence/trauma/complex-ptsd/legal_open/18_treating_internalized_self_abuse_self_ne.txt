00001	   TREATING INTERNALIZED SELF-ABUSE & SELF-NEGLECT 
00002	 
00003	  This paper describes the role of relational psychotherapy in remediating 
00004	key developmental traumas and arrests in DTD [Developmental Trauma 
00005	Disorder] and CPTSD [Complex Post Traumatic Stress Disorder].  My long 
00006	experience as a trauma therapist convinces me that the most destructive 
00007	childhood traumas are relational. 
00008	 
00009	  Here are two of the most damaging. Firstly, children of traumatizing 
00010	caretakers are raised with overwhelming amounts of contempt and neglect 
00011	which robs them of their birthright to be copiously loved and supported, and 
00012	suffocates them in an abandonment depression of shame, fear and 
00013	hopelessness.  
00014	 
00015	 Secondly, such children respond to being continuously traumatized by 
00016	learning to relate to themselves with the same constant abuse and neglect 
00017	as their parents. They develop a self-traumatizing relationship with 
00018	themselves.  
00019	 
00020	  The way out is to be therapeutically guided into reawakening and 
00021	restoring their developmentally arrested instincts of self-compassion and 
00022	self-protection in a safe relationship that models caring and support. 
00023	  
00024	  
00025	DTD [Developmental Trauma Disorder]
00026	 is another name for 
00027	CPTSD 
00028	[Complex PTSD] 
00029	with an emphasis on childhood traumatization. It was 
00030	coined by Bessel van der Kolk to get CPTSD reconsidered for acceptance 
00031	in the DSM via a new name. Although it was rejected, CPTSD is now listed 
00032	in the ICD-11, the Global standard for diagnostic health information.]  
00033	 
00034	  DTD highlights the severe developmental trauma that results when a 
00035	child’s caregiving system is rife with physical, emotional, and educational 
00036	maltreatment and neglect. Herein, these two labels will be used together 
00037	interchangeably, because in my experience, CPTSD is basically what DTD 
00038	invariably morphs into after childhood.  
00039	 
00040	 
00041	TRAUMA BLOCKED SELF-COMPASSION AND SELF-PROTECTION 
00042	 
00043	  Dysfunctional parents traumatize children with their ongoing abusive and 
00044	neglectful parenting practices. Such treatment induces DTD/CPTSD, as 
00045	traumatizing parents so thoroughly decimate the instincts of self-
00046	compassion and self-protection in their children, that adult survivors of it 
00047	remain hamstrung by anxiety, excruciating shame, debilitating depression 
00048	and troublesome relationships with themselves and others. These 
00049	developmental aberrations can endure until their dying days if they are not 
00050	helped to recover from these effects. 
00051	 
00052	  The fundamental cause of DTD/CPTSD is the lack of a supportive and 
00053	loving relationship with any caretaker. When this occurs, continuously 
00054	abused/neglected children fail to develop a caring and supportive 
00055	relationship with themselves. This in turn robs them of their ability to access 
00056	and develop the innate self-esteem that they possess at birth.  
00057	 
00058	  Moreover, their 
00059	self-compassion,
00060	 their innate capacity to feel empathy for 
00061	themselves during difficult times, quickly dwindles and disappears when 
00062	they never receive sympathy from their caretakers. This is especially so 
00063	when they are also punished and shamed for crying and feeling sorry 
00064	[sorrow] for themselves.  
00065	 
00066	  The same is true for self-protection, the healthy instinct innate in every 
00067	child to angrily say “no” to abuse and neglect. Dysfunctional parents 
00068	typically punish children harshly for angrily crying or complaining about 
00069	unfairness. Over time, children’s self-protective reflexes are traumatically 
00070	repressed and lost to the unconscious.  
00071	 
00072	  Even worse, when such children are not allowed to protect themselves 
00073	against injustice, they soon lose their ability to defend themselves in all 
00074	their relationships. They unprotestingly allow others to treat them as poorly 
00075	as their original caretakers. They come to implicitly believe that abuse 
00076	and/or neglect are part of the price tag of belonging. 
00077	 
00078	  To the degree that their instincts of self-compassion and self-protection 
00079	become inoperable, DTD/CPTSD survivors unconsciously forfeit their rights 
00080	and needs to be treated fairly in current and future relationships. They 
00081	never learn to form boundaries or set limits with antagonistic others, and 
00082	become ripe targets for relational exploitation.  
00083	 
00084	THE INSTALLMENT OF THE TOXIC INNER CRITIC 
00085	 
00086	  Children instinctively mimic their parents. They learn to stymie and punish 
00087	themselves just as their parents do. They become excessively judgmental 
00088	of themselves because in their families there is no such thing as a 
00089	harmless mistake, a faux pas or “small potatoes” miscue. A mispronounced 
00090	word, a fleeting facial expression or a less than happy mood can regularly 
00091	trigger verbal, emotional or physical punishment and rejection. 
00092	 
00093	  In copying their parents, traumatized children become preoccupied with 
00094	their shortcomings. They strive to become absolutely mistake-free, 
00095	eventually only able to see what is imperfect or lacking in themselves. 
00096	Before long, they can only negatively-notice themselves.  
00097	 
00098	  Over time, negative self-noticing grows into a toxic inner critic … a 
00099	perversion of the superego … which goes viral in their psyches and leaves 
00100	little or no room for the development of a healthy, user-friendly self-
00101	supportive ego…a self-compassionate and self-protective ego. 
00102	 
00103	  Even worse, their toxic inner critic finally coopts their creativity, and 
00104	invents erroneous, and often paradoxical self-criticisms that can last a 
00105	lifetime. I have too often witnessed workaholic clients trashing themselves 
00106	as lazy, prodigies branding themselves as stupid, beauties declaring 
00107	themselves ugly, codependents judging themselves selfish, and empaths 
00108	bemoaning their cold-heartedness. 
00109	     
00110	  Perpetual parental negative-noticing causes them to constantly attack, shame 
00111	and scare themselves, which in turn, as teens and adults, often makes them 
00112	brand themselves as “crazy”, and plagues them with suicidal ideation. 
00113	   
00114	  The toxic inner critic typically becomes an inner bully. It relentlessly uses a 
00115	multimodal attack of extreme perfectionism to blame the child for not being 
00116	perfect. It also enforces this perfectionism with frightening endangerment 
00117	visualizations of an overwhelmingly hostile and rejecting future. [In Chapter 9 of 
00118	my book: Complex PTSD: From Surviving to Thriving, I describe the inner critic’s 
00119	14 most common habitual, life-narrowing processes of perfectionism and 
00120	endangerment. I also include thought- and perspective-changing responses to 
00121	combat these attacks.] 
00122	 
00123	  Children eventually grow up and leave home, but the inner bully goes with 
00124	them. It systematically punishes any behavior, thought, body-flaw, feeling, 
00125	healthy aspiration, etc., that is less than perfect, and like the parents that 
00126	created it routinely scorns any kind of approval that comes their way. 
00127	Whenever they are not at 100%, they frequently spiral down, deeper and 
00128	deeper into excruciating fear and self-disgust.   
00129	 
00130	  Compounding this, adult survivors are also often unconsciously attracted 
00131	to other bullies in the wider society who further traumatize them via what 
00132	Freud called repetition compulsion. Survivors automatically accept their 
00133	bad behavior because their childhoods taught them that it is too dangerous 
00134	to protest unfairness.  
00135	 
00136	  In the worst cases, some untreated survivors can end up being captured 
00137	by narcissistic bullies who psychologically enslave them. Others amass a 
00138	history of failed persecutory relations that morph their inner critics into a 
00139	posse of invisible bully-ghosts led by their internalized scornful parental 
00140	sheriffs. Still others retreat into self-isolation to avoid the frightening threat 
00141	of relationships altogether. And most tragically, some model on their 
00142	narcissistic caretakers and become bullies themselves, making DTD and 
00143	CPTSD self-generating legacies. 
00144	 
00145	  In almost all cases, survivors of DTD are afflicted with huge amount of 
00146	social anxiety. Such survivors can spend their whole lives without the ability 
00147	to let in any kind of interpersonal comfort. They suffer intense alienation, 
00148	tortured by self-hate and self-disgust for failing at the impossible goal of 
00149	becoming perfect. This is the tragic meaning of Alice Miller’s book title: 
00150	Prisoners of Childhood, [aka, The Drama of the Gifted Child]. 
00151	 
00152	  Such is the fate that ensues from a developmental lack of parental 
00153	kindness, and from parental taboos against normal, healthy complaining 
00154	[aka, self-protection]. Thus, psychotherapy for CPTSD requires an 
00155	approach that teaches and models self-compassionate and self-protective 
00156	relating to oneself.  
00157	 
00158	  {It is important to note here that this work does not aim to eliminate 
00159	healthy self-criticism, which is a positive function of a superego that 
00160	develops healthily. To the degree that toxic self-criticism is diminished, then 
00161	to that same degree does healthy self-criticism become a kind and gentle 
00162	voice of self-questioning and self-improvement.

00164	REVIVING SELF-COMPASSION AND SELF-PROTECTION  
00165	 
00166	Reawakening and nurturing the human instinct to care for and protect 
00167	oneself through thick and thin is therefore a crucial goal in treatment. Life is 
00168	incredibly difficult when someone cannot access their instincts of self-
00169	compassion and self-protection. Moreover, these instincts rarely become 
00170	reactivated without the aid of a therapeutic relationship. This is perhaps the 
00171	most tragic developmental trauma in DTD  
00172	 
00173	  Therapists can help survivors to reawaken their instincts to kind and, 
00174	when necessary, fierce allies for themselves.  
00175	 
00176	  Reviving these key elements of self-support usually begins with 
00177	psychoeducation about their destruction in childhood. A relational and 
00178	reparenting perspective is most effective in this, and helps to gradually 
00179	abate the terribly fearful, loveless and shame-saturated state of the 
00180	survivor’s psyche. 
00181	 
00182	  I use my enhancements of the reparenting perspectives and techniques of 
00183	John Bradshaw to address these issues. My aim is to generously and 
00184	appropriately meet crucial unmet parenting needs. These include positively 
00185	noticing clients, treating them kindly, emphasizing that they are essentially 
00186	worthy, and helping them to learn how to recognize and defend themselves 
00187	from toxic relationships…including the punishing relationship with 
00188	themselves that was enculturated by their traumatizing parents.

00190	REAWAKENING DEVELOPMENTALLY ARRESTED SELF-COMPASSION 
00191	 
00192	  Every human being is born with an instinct for self-compassion. Thriving 
00193	depends on having empathy for oneself during life’s inevitable difficult 
00194	times, and on treating oneself graciously during life’s unavoidable 
00195	emotional ups and downs.  
00196	 
00197	      
00198	The Therapeutic Power of Grieving 
00199	 
00200	   At its most powerful, self-compassion is a “good cry”. An egosyntonic cry 
00201	is the ultimate anxiety-releaser. Tears can release body tension and fear, 
00202	and shift a person into a relaxed parasympathetic state. Metabolizing 
00203	physical or emotional pain through crying comes naturally to us all, unless 
00204	or until it is interfered with.  
00205	 
00206	  Crying should be at the top of every list of stress-releasing techniques for 
00207	working through any type of past or present suffering; and crying is 
00208	especially effective when it is interspersed with angering about current or 
00209	past unfairness. Angry crying is our earliest language. Picture and hear the 
00210	signals coming from a distressed infant calling out for help. 
00211	 
00212	  On innumerable occasions, I have witnessed clients verbally agonizing 
00213	about endless recirculating worries until my empathic interjections elicited 
00214	them into having a good cry; so often, their obsessive catastrophizing then 
00215	evaporated with their tears.  
00216	 
00217	  On one occasion, a client told me as her tears subsided: “I can’t believe I 
00218	was just so suicidal; and now I’m looking at that gorgeous painting on your 
00219	wall, feeling dazzled by its beauty! Where did all that pain go?”  
00220	 
00221	  I say: “It seems to me that crying released your painful feelings of fear. 
00222	Without the emotional release of tears, your fear wafts up into your left 
00223	brain and becomes cognitive, creating fearful processes like drasticizing 
00224	and hypochondriasizing. I think tears release fear -- the emotional fuel of 
00225	fearful thinking, and allows us to reconnect with our abilities to cope with 
00226	our problems.’” 
00227	 
00228	  I have had a number of stints as a suicide hotline counselor and trainer, 
00229	and invariably, when I empathized enough with a caller’s pain, the caller 
00230	cried deeply and their suicidality resolved along with the release of their 
00231	tears; so too with innumerable clients in session voiding their abject 
00232	hopelessness along with their tears. 
00233	 
00234	  While self-compassion is innate, children need to experience consistent 
00235	parental compassion when they cry to ensure that their self-empathy 
00236	matures and remains automatic. A caretaker’s compassion also helps 
00237	children to internalize a kind way of treating themselves.  
00238	 
00239	  Children born in CPTSD-inducing families never or rarely receive 
00240	empathy. Consistently critical and/or unresponsive parenting replaces 
00241	fledgling self-compassion with toxic shame. Traumatologist John Bradshaw 
00242	coined the term toxic shame to describe the soul- and spirit-deadening 
00243	processes that traumatizing parents use to obliterate their children’s self-
00244	supportive reflexes, typically to make them easier to control. Toxic shame 
00245	is the excruciating emotional byproduct of the pathologizing inner critic. 
00246	 
00247	     Inner Critic, Perfectionism and Toxic Shame 
00248	 
00249	  Neglect alone can install toxic shame as deeply as abuse. Neglect [verbal, 
00250	spiritual and emotional] occurs when parents fail to interact positively in any 
00251	way with their children, when they never notice anything good about them, 
00252	when they turn a blind eye toward their accomplishments, when they never 
00253	show delight in the daily miracles of their developing self-expression and 
00254	intelligence, and when they don’t regularly interact with them in a loving 
00255	and light-hearted way. 
00256	 
00257	  Extended neglect, like abuse, drives most children to automatically 
00258	embrace perfectionism. Over time, they consciously or unconsciously adopt 
00259	such tenets as: “If only I don’t mess up, need anything, complain, interrupt 
00260	them…then, maybe they’ll pay attention to me…maybe they’ll like 
00261	me…maybe they’ll stop wishing they didn’t have me!”  
00262	 
00263	  Consequently, such children only attend to themselves negatively. They 
00264	increasingly obsess about lacking any value, about not being good enough, 
00265	about being fatally flawed. They soon learn that showing disappointment, 
00266	sadness, and normal mood-shifts invites parental attack or shunning; and 
00267	showing anger about being hurt or treated unfairly is especially dangerous 
00268	and typically greeted with the most intense punishment or abandonment.  
00269	 
00270	  This forces children to embrace emotional perfectionism. When they 
00271	cannot pump up any happiness, they flounder for long periods in the 
00272	alienated agony of toxic shame and self-hate. They come to believe that 
00273	emotional pain is their fatal flaw. Over time, they often become compulsive 
00274	in their attempts to do anything they can to suppress, numb or distract 
00275	themselves from their less-than-happy feelings.  
00276	 
00277	  It is important to note here that addressing a client’s self-pathologizing 
00278	view of their dysphoric emotions is often long-term work This is because 
00279	the most damaging emotional trauma for most survivors occurred in 
00280	toddlerhood, and most people do not form memories that early in life. They 
00281	typically have trouble believing that their childhood could have been 
00282	traumatizing enough to cause chronic shame, fear and depression…or to 
00283	give them a toxic inner critic. 
00284	 
00285	   Relationally Supporting Healthy Emotional Development 
00286	 
00287	  Effective therapy can reawaken self-compassion. To revive clients’ 
00288	dormant self-kindness, therapists must relate compassionately to them. 
00289	They must invite and make it safe for survivors to verbally and emotionally 
00290	ventilate about their pain, past and present. 
00291	 
00292	  Human beings instinctively feel sad and mad when they are hurt. They 
00293	cannot fully metabolize their pain without crying and angering about it. 
00294	Survivors need a compassionate, validating witness to release their 
00295	ungrieved traumatic childhood pain. They typically need to cry and angrily 
00296	protest the unfairness of it many, many times on many, many occasions to 
00297	work through all the intricacies of their abuse and neglect. 
00298	 
00299	  A therapist can become the first person ever to help the survivor remedy 
00300	these developmental arrests. The therapist can do this by allowing the 
00301	survivor to discover the relief that comes from self-compassionate crying 
00302	and self-protective angering.  
00303	 
00304	  Tragically, the therapist who does not validate and empathize with the 
00305	survivor’s pain may become the survivor’s last unsuccessful attempt to 
00306	establish trust and find reparative emotional acceptance from another 
00307	human being; or they may add their name to a list of helping professionals 
00308	who brought on further shame by failing to understand the client’s plight of 
00309	being helpless against the onslaughts of their toxic critic. For therapists 
00310	who have not experienced ongoing childhood trauma, it may be very 
00311	difficult to understand and sympathize with the deeply entrenched, 
00312	merciless and relentless nature of the CPTSD-engendered critic.  
00313	 
00314	  Relationally addressing these arrests in clients helps them to discover the 
00315	comfort that comes from being fully seen and accepted in all aspects of 
00316	themselves. When they get enough experience of this, survivors can learn 
00317	to metabolize their pain by verbally and emotionally ventilating it elsewhere 
00318	with safe enough others. They can then gradually discover that there is 
00319	nothing inside them that needs to be hated, pathologized, or self-
00320	destructively avoided. Even better, they can experience the fact that 
00321	intimacy can deepen to otherwise unavailable depths when two people 
00322	commiserate about their pain. 
00323	 
00324	  Therapists can greatly enhance this process by modeling an acceptance 
00325	of their own imperfections. They can use their own vulnerability to 
00326	encourage clients to venture into the fear and shame that encrusts their 
00327	less-than-perfect experiences. A common way that I do this is to normalize 
00328	unhelpful therapeutic interventions on my part by compassionately 
00329	apologizing for them without shame or self-contempt. 
00330	 
00331	  For over forty years, I have seen how a relational approach facilitates 
00332	survivors to work toward becoming a loyal ally to themselves no matter 
00333	what they are suffering. Over and over, I am told that my vulnerability is the 
00334	thing that helped them the most to shrink their shame, to find comfort in 
00335	crying, and to use anger in a way that makes them feel safer in the world 
00336	and in their own skin.

00345	REVIVING DEVELOPMENTALLY ARRESTED SELF-PROTECTION  
00346	 
00347	  This is typically the most difficult development arrest to address, 
00348	especially as so many psychological and spiritual perspectives villainize 
00349	anger in an all-or-nothing way…in a way that conflates loving with never  
00350	being angry. Ironically, when survivors learn how to love themselves 
00351	sufficiently, the instinct to fight for themselves readily awakens. 
00352	   
00353	  Suppressing a child’s self-protective reflexes typically takes some doing 
00354	as children instinctively complain about abuse and neglect. “No!” and 
00355	“That’s not fair!” appear very early in most toddlers’ vocabularies. Most 
00356	traumatizing parents, however, reserve especially intense punishment for 
00357	their children’s attempts to protect themselves against their abuse. They 
00358	quickly extinguish the child’s drive to stand up against injustice. 
00359	   
00360	Contempt is the weapon of choice of many overcontrolling parents. 
00361	Contempt is an emotional cocktail of intimidation and disgust that drowns 
00362	children’s self-expression in fear and shame, respectively. Such extreme 
00363	rejection eventually causes children to identify with their aggressors [Freud] 
00364	and beat their parents to the punch by automatically blaming [shaming] 
00365	themselves for any parental upset, no matter how unjust and unfair.  
00366	 
00367	  What a huge endangering loss this is, as an angry “that’s-not-fair” is an 
00368	emotionally intelligent survival response to abuse or neglect at any time in 
00369	life. How awful it is that this phrase is almost universally scorned by parents 
00370	in our culture, even those who are not otherwise abusive! 
00371	 
00372	  As noted earlier, most ongoingly traumatized children adapt to toxic 
00373	shaming by consciously or unconsciously embracing perfectionism as their 
00374	salvation fantasy. Typically, this is not the clean-the-bathroom-floor-with-a-
00375	toothbrush kind of perfectionism, but rather a contentment-draining self-
00376	dissatisfaction that infects every level of their being.  
00377	 
00378	  Most survivors become contemptuous of their own looks, their speech, 
00379	their moods, their energy levels, their actions and inactions. Everything 
00380	they do or say must be absolutely beyond reproach or they spiral down into 
00381	perseverating self-castigation. As the impossible goal of becoming mistake-
00382	free eludes them, they are prone to seeing themselves as nothing but one 
00383	giant mistake. 
00384	 
00385	Attaining Outer and Inner Safety 
00386	 
00387	  As Judith Herman wisely wrote, the therapist’s first priority in treatment is 
00388	to guide clients into breaking contact with abusers in the outer world. Once 
00389	good enough safety in the outer world has been achieved, however, 
00390	creating safety in the clients’ own mind is essential to further CPTSD 
00391	recovery.  
00392	 
00393	   At such a juncture, attaining inner safety must be prioritized because the 
00394	critic, the 24/7 manager of an untreated survivor’s psyche, typically 
00395	becomes the foremost triggering mechanism of emotional flashbacks. Over 
00396	and over, emotional flashbacks are instigated by inner critic attacks which 
00397	demean and frighten survivors back into the childhood abandonment 
00398	depression.  
00399	 
00400	  The critic can use any wrinkle to drive the survivor back into childhood’s 
00401	hellish toxic mood of fear, shame and hopelessness. Even worse, the 
00402	uncontested critic, the unrelenting negative-noticer of the psyche, keeps on 
00403	firing during a flashback driving survivors deeper and deeper into this 
00404	abandonment mélange, often entrapping them there for the best part of 
00405	days, months, years and even lifetimes if they remain untreated. [Chapter 8 
00406	of my CPTSD book expands on emotional flashbacks and contains 13 
00407	steps for managing and mitigating them.] 
00408	 
00409	  DTD/CPTSD survivors were inculcated with a self-sabotaging critic when 
00410	they were too young and powerless to fight it. Until they learn to intensely 
00411	rebuff it, most survivors rarely get much traction in rescuing themselves 
00412	from its incessant belittling of them.  
00413	 
00414	  In this vein, the renowned developmental psychologist, Erik Eriksen, 
00415	offered us a powerful tool when he said: “… shame is healthy blame turned 
00416	against the self.” A savvy, patient therapist can turn this emotional math 
00417	equation into an antidote for toxic shame, by repeatedly encouraging the 
00418	survivor to blame the critic and the installers of the critic for inculcating 
00419	them with a self-perpetuating mental program that constantly stokes up 
00420	self-alienation and self-punishment. 
00421	 
00422	  When self-protective angering against the critic is practiced frequently 
00423	enough over some months, it begins to morph toxic shame back into 
00424	healthy and appropriate blaming of the abuse and neglect that forced the 
00425	survivor as a child to become self-contemptuous.  
00426	 
00427	  This, however, is no fast fix; yet, when this practice is combined with the 
00428	help of many self-compassionate tears, survivors gradually increase their 
00429	ability to rescue themselves from getting stranded in emotional flashbacks. 
00430	 
00431	  For years, I got nowhere with simple cognitive thought-stopping. In fact, 
00432	this failure moved me even deeper into toxic shame, as all the other 
00433	therapists around me claimed that it helped them greatly. Eventually, this 
00434	frustrated me so much that I started to charge my thought stopping with 
00435	anger, and religiously fought my critic innumerable times a day for months 
00436	and, lo and behold, I finally started to see that my critic was indeed 
00437	gradually diminishing.  
00438	 
00439	  Therapists can accelerate survivors’ recovery by persistently identifying 
00440	inner critic attacks, and by encouraging clients to be angry about how 
00441	childhood trauma turned them into persecutors of themselves. Therapists 
00442	can then teach survivors how to angrily fight against inner critic messages 
00443	that trigger and trap them into painful flashbacks. Clients can learn to do 
00444	this in a private place, or quietly in the privacy of their own psyches, or 
00445	actively out loud in sessions, or with a safe and supportive other in 
00446	witnessed role plays.  
00447	 
00448	  Therapists can also enhance this process by sharing their own 
00449	commiserative anger about all bullying, gaslighting and scapegoating, 
00450	whether the target was/is the client, they themselves, or anyone being 
00451	controlled by another’s contempt. Ultimately, this anger can then be used to 
00452	muffle and shrink the inner bully and its control over their psyches.  
00453	   
00454	  Shrinking the critic is most effective when survivors also anger, even rage, 
00455	at the critic as the proxy of their early “care-givers”. With a great deal of 
00456	ongoing practice, angrily ordering the internalized parents and the toxic 
00457	critic to: “Shut up”, “Get out of my head”, and even “F*** off”, etc., 
00458	progressively shrinks the critic and makes more room for the healthy self-
00459	protective ego to evolve out of its arrested development. [I give detailed 
00460	practical instructions on how to safely express anger without hurting 
00461	oneself or anyone else in Chapters 5 & 7 of my book: The Tao of Fully 
00462	Feeling.] 
00463	 
00464	Recovering the self-protective instinct can be increasing accelerated by 
00465	therapists sharing their own empathic anger about how clients were trained 
00466	to become their own worst enemies. Therapists can do this authentically by 
00467	remembering and invoking the healthy anger they feel/felt in similar 
00468	situations, or on seeing someone being bullied in a movie, or on the street, 
00469	or in untold videos of scapegoating politicians like Donald Trump shame-
00470	blaming someone else for their mistakes and deficiencies. 
00471	 
00472	  As someone who has had to work hard to recover a healthy sense of self-
00473	protection, I now instinctively feel angry at the abuser when I hear a client 
00474	talk about being bullied. I believe it is instinctual to want to protect people 
00475	we care about from cruel treatment. And, when a client tells me about their 
00476	childhood abuse in a session, I often wish I could go back in time to help 
00477	them by stopping the bullying.  
00478	  With most clients, I work gradually towards sharing my feelings of being 
00479	angry at their inner critic and at those who installed it. It may take many 
00480	months…sometimes years… to help clients see how their parents’ 
00481	abuse/neglect turned their superegos into inner bullies, and to see that this 
00482	was at the expense of the development of their healthy ego: the user-
00483	friendly manager and protector of their psyches.  
00484	 
00485	  Once they begin to reverberate with this, I gradually encourage them to 
00486	identify and get angry about all the specific ways they were forced to only 
00487	see themselves negatively. The more their natural anger about this 
00488	surfaces, the more they grow in their ability to morph their shame into 
00489	blame. [I have also been delighted to receive numerous emails from

00503	inner heat and rage as needed. As this process gradually dethrones the

00508	    Combining Fighting The Inner Critic with Self-Compassion 
00509	 
00510	  With enough practice of feeling and emotionally expressing the instincts of 
00511	self-protection and self-compassion, a powerful two-step process can be 
00512	taught to the survivor…one that will then often evolve naturally in the client. 
00513	 
00514	   In step one, the intrusive critic is met with angry resistance until it is at 
00515	least somewhat muffled, and then in step two, the survivor switches to 
00516	positively-noticing and empathically expressing self-support to the 
00517	traumatized self and to the inner child at various stages of development.   
00518	 
00519	  Here is an example of this: “I love you. I am so sorry that you have 
00520	suffered so much from this toxic critic. I love you and I am on your side no 
00521	matter what. I love you and I want the best for you. You are a good, worthy 
00522	person, and I will always welcome your tears. They will help us to 
00523	increasingly release our fear and shame…to feel safer and more supported 
00524	in the world.”” 
00525	 
00526	  With further practice, it is not uncommon for either step to spontaneously, 
00527	elicit the other step. I am especially moved when the cathartic process of 
00528	limit-setting with the critic leads clients quite naturally into self-
00529	compassionate crying and grieving for all the instances when they suffered 
00530	helplessly from the attacks of their parents and, by extension, their critics.  
00531	 
00532	Especially therapeutic sessions often involve vacillating back and forth 
00533	between crying and angering. Once again, when we are hurt, it is normal 
00534	and instinctive to feel both sad and mad, and we can learn to feel and 
00535	express this sadness and anger in a way that is therapeutic and restorative 
00536	of our ability to be unrelentingly self-supportive.  
00537	   
00538	  As clients increasingly reclaim their instincts of self-compassion and self-
00539	protection, we can then help them to emotionally empower the CBT 
00540	techniques of thought-stopping and perspective-substitution. This is 
00541	especially important, as I rarely see anyone diminish their toxic critic until 
00542	they anger at it a great deal.  
00543	 
00544	  Similarly, recovering genuine self-compassion seems to depend on 
00545	numerous experiences of feeling and crying out real sorrow about all the 
00546	unjust childhood experiences that created one’s CPTSD.  
00547	   
00548	  As healthy anger and tears become more accessible, these self-
00549	supportive emotional responses can then be used to triage any new- or 
00550	flashback-injury to their self-esteem. When I witness survivors coming to 
00551	their own aid at such times, it often seems like they are cauterizing their 
00552	negative noticing with the heat of their anger, and replacing it with deeply 
00553	felt, often teary, affirmation of their own attributes and essential worthiness.  
00554	 
00555	  Over time, these processes steadily shrink the critic. Self-compassion and 
00556	self-protection become ongoing, self-perpetuating developmental 
00557	processes. Tears for the self morph more readily into self-empathy and 
00558	kindness; and angering at being brainwashed into self-hate and self-disgust 
00559	so early in life typically morphs into self-protective qualities such as 
00560	courage, assertiveness and initiative.  
00561	 
00562	  Concurrently, anxiety and unnecessary fear continuously abate. Toxic 
00563	shame melts. Their creative self-expression is freed to grow and develop. 
00564	Clients grow in their abilities to make boundaries and set limits around 
00565	unfairness. And, this in turn allows them to recognize and avoid abusive 
00566	people, and choose friends who are capable of mutually supportive and 
00567	respectful relationships. This is especially true when they experience their 
00568	therapy as genuinely safe and caring. 
00569	 
00570	  This also helps survivors of CPTSD to manage their emotional flashbacks 
00571	more effectively. With enough practice, their instinctual anger awakens 
00572	more readily to fight and protect themselves from inner critic attacks. Anger 
00573	about the gross injustice of having family-inflicted DTD quickly arises as a 
00574	fierce intuitive rage to reject self-abuse. When this process fully matures, 
00575	triggered survivors feel outraged about the fact that their psyches were 
00576	entranced to turn on a dime against them, over and over, for every faux 
00577	pas, mood change, energy lull or less than perfect utterance. 
00578	 
00579	  [Interestingly, I have worked with quite a few Christian and Buddhist 
00580	trauma survivors who initially balked at the notion of angering at the critic, 
00581	dismissing it as unspiritual. For many, however, my psychoeducation 
00582	gradually de-minimized their denial about the calamity of their childhoods, 
00583	and eventually opened then up to angering at the critic. Sooner or later, 
00584	they were quickly and happily moved to start aggressively setting limits with
