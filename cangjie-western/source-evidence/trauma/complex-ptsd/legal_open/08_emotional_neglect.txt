00001	              Emotional Neglect and Complex PTSD 
00002	                         By Pete Walker 
00003	 
00004	This article highlights the prodigious role that emotional neglect 
00005	plays in childhood trauma, and how it alone can create Complex 
00006	PTSD. It begins by extensively examining the processes of denial 
00007	and minimization that blunt our awareness about childhood 
00008	trauma. Denial is first explored in relationship to abuse, especially 
00009	verbal and emotional abuse, which then sets the stage for a more 
00010	complete explication of the trauma of emotional neglect.  
00011	Denial about the deleterious effects of childhood abandonment 
00012	seriously delimits our ability to recover. Continuous emotional 
00013	neglect turns the child’s psyche into a quagmire of emptiness, fear 
00014	and shame - a quagmire that she will, as an adult, frequently 
00015	flashback into until she understands and works through the 
00016	wretchedness of her childhood. Without such understanding, her 
00017	crucial, unmet needs for safe and comforting, human connection 
00018	will continue to cause her an enormous amount of unnecessary 
00019	suffering.  
00020	Denial and minimization. Recovery from ptsd correlates with an 
00021	individual’s ability to understand on deep impactful levels how 
00022	derelict her parents’ were in their duty to nurture and protect her. 
00023	The individual needs to get that emotional flashbacks are direct 
00024	messages from her child-self about how seriously her parents hurt 
00025	and injured her. As denial is significantly deconstructed, the 
00026	recoveree feels genuine compassion for the child she was. This in 
00027	turn motivates her to engage the healing process of identifying and 
00028	addressing the specific wounds of her childhood. Over time she 
00029	becomes aware of her specific abandonment picture and the pattern 
00030	of physical, spiritual, verbal and emotional abuse and/or neglect 
00031	that she experienced. [Chapter 8 of my book, The Tao of Fully 
00032	Feeling, provides guidelines for assessing your particular pattern]. 
00033	Confronting denial is no small task. Children so need to believe 
00034	that their parents love and care for them, that they will deny and 
00035	minimize away evidence of the most egregious neglect and abuse. 
00036	De-minimization is a crucial aspect of confronting denial. It is the 
00037	process by which the individual deconstructs the defense of  
00038	making light of his childhood trauma. The lifelong process of de-
00039	minimizing the impact of childhood trauma is like peeling a very 
00040	slippery and caustic onion. The outer layer for some is the stark 
00041	physical evidence of abuse, e.g., sexual abuse or excessive 
00042	corporal punishment. In a perversely ironic way, my parents’ 
00043	physical abuse of me as a child was a blessing for it was so blatant 
00044	that my attempts to suppress, rationalize, make light of and laugh it 
00045	off lost their power in adolescence, and I was able to see my father 
00046	for the bully that he was. [Seeing my defensively idealized 
00047	mother’s abusiveness came much later].  
00048	Identifying my father’s behavior as abusive eventually helped me 
00049	become aware of less dramatic aspects of my parents’ oppression, 
00050	and I subsequently discovered the verbal and emotional abuse 
00051	layer of the onion of my childhood abandonment. 
00052	Verbal and Emotional Abuse. The fact that verbal and emotional 
00053	abuse can be traumatic is lost on many childhood trauma victims. 
00054	Many never learn to validate its crippling effects. They never 
00055	accurately assign current time suffering to it. Attempts to 
00056	acknowledge it are typically blindsided with thoughts that it was 
00057	nothing compared to kids who were repeatedly beaten – who had it 
00058	worse. Yet for me, and many of my clients, verbal and emotional 
00059	abuse was much more injurious than our physical abuse.  
00060	Being ongoingly assaulted with critical words systematically 
00061	destroys innate self-esteem and replaces it with a prevailing 
00062	consciousness of toxic self-criticism. Even worse, words that are 
00063	emotionally poisoned with contempt [a deadly cocktail of 
00064	intimidation and disgust] infuse the child with fear and toxic shame 
00065	respectively. Fear and shame condition him to refrain from asking 
00066	for attention, from expressing himself in ways that draw attention, 
00067	and before long from seeking any kind of help or connection at all. 
00068	Unrelenting criticism, especially when it is ground in with parental 
00069	rage and scorn, is so injurious that it changes the structure of the 
00070	child’s brain.  
00071	Here is a theoretical model of this. Repeated messages of disdain 
00072	are internalized and adopted by the child, who repeats them over 
00073	and over to himself. Incessant repetitions result in the construction 
00074	of thick neural pathways of self-hate and self-disgust. Over time a 
00075	self-hate response attaches to more and more of the child’s 
00076	cognitions, feelings and behaviors. Eventually, any inclination 
00077	toward authentic or vulnerable self-expression activates internal 
00078	neural networks of self-loathing. The child is forced to exist in a 
00079	crippling state of self-attack, which eventually becomes equivalent 
00080	to a state of full-fledged self-abandonment. The ability to support 
00081	or nurture himself or take his own side in anyway is decimated. 
00082	With ongoing parental reinforcement, these neural pathways 
00083	expand into a large complex network that becomes an Inner Critic 
00084	that dominates mental activity. This critic elaborates myriad 
00085	programs of self-rejecting perfectionism and paints the psyche with 
00086	the endangerment scenarios that I describe in my articles on 
00087	“Shrinking The Critic”. Until these programs are effectively 
00088	deconstructed, the individual typically lives in varying degrees of 
00089	emotional flashback much of the time. 
00090	1
00091	The verbal and emotional layer of the abuse onion has myriad sub-
00092	layers of minimization which must be confronted in the long 
00093	difficult disengagement of one’s identity from the toxic critic. I 
00094	have heard clients jokingly repeat numerous versions of this over 
00095	and over: “I know I’m hard on myself, but if I don’t constantly 
00096	kick my own ass, I’ll be more of a loser than I already am.” 
00097	 
00098	 A childhood rife with verbal and emotional abuse often creates an 
00099	identification with the critic that is so pervasive, that it is as if the 
00100	critic is the whole identity. Disidentification from the critic is the 
00101	fight of a lifetime, and for a long time there is a great pull to 
00102	collapse back into the old habit of self-blame. Ironically this self-
00103	hate can constellate around the self-judgment that one is especially 
00104	defective because she cannot simply banish the critic. [Typical 
00105	toxic, all-or-none thinking from the critic]. Sadly, many survivors 
00106	give up before recognizing the myriad subtle ways the critic 
00107	tortures them. Yet, there is no more noble recovery battle than that 
00108	which gradually frees the psyche from critic dominance. Until this 
00109	happens to a significant degree, there is minimal development of 
00110	the healthy, user-friendly ego.  
00111	Let us look now at how emotional neglect alone creates a psyche-
00112	dominating Critic. 
00113	Emotional Neglect: The Core Wound in Complex PTSD 
00114	Minimization about the debilitating consequences of a childhood 
00115	rife with emotional neglect is at the core of the PTSD denial onion. 
00116	Our recovery efforts are impeded until we understand how much of 
00117	our suffering constellates around early emotional abandonment – 
00118	around the great emptiness that springs from the dearth of parental 
00119	loving interest and engagement, and around the harrowing 
00120	experience of being small and powerless while growing up in a 
00121	world where there is no-one who’s got your back. Many survivors 
00122	never get to discover and work through the wounds that correlate 
00123	with this level, because they over-assign their suffering to overt 
00124	abuse and never get to the core issue of emotional abandonment. 
00125	As stated above, this is especially true when they dismissively 
00126	compare their trauma to those who were abused more noticeably 
00127	and more dramatically. [This is particularly ironic in light of the 
00128	fact that some individuals can  suffer a modicum of active abuse 
00129	without developing PTSD, if there is one caretaker who does not 
00130	emotionally neglect them]. 
00131	Traumatic emotional neglect occurs when a child does not have a 
00132	single parent or caretaker to whom she can turn in times of need or 
00133	danger, and when she does not have anyone for an extended period 
00134	of time who is a relatively consistent source of comfort and 
00135	protection.  Growing up emotionally neglected is like nearly dying 
00136	of thirst just outside the fenced off fountain of a parent’s kindness 
00137	and interest. Emotional neglect makes children feel worthless, 
00138	unlovable and excruciatingly empty, with a hunger that gnaws 
00139	deeply at the center of their being, leaving them starving for human 
00140	warmth and comfort - a hunger that often morphs over time into an 
00141	insatiable appetite for substances and/or addictive processes. [I 
00142	find it noteworthy that denial processes about early abandonment 
00143	often morph later in life to the minimizing operations that some 
00144	survivors use to rationalize their substance and process addictions. 
00145	While addictions are often understandable, misplaced attempts to 
00146	regulate painful emotional flashbacks, they become increasingly 
00147	self-destructive when an individual is old enough to learn a 
00148	healthier flashback management regimen. Accordingly, excessive 
00149	eating, spending, drinking, drugging, sexing, working or 
00150	dissociating, are not only desperate attempts to distract from inner 
00151	pain, but also counterproductive efforts to attain an ersatz form of 
00152	human comfort and soothing.  And while many recoverees 
00153	eventually come to see their substance or process addictions as 
00154	problematic, many also minimize their deleterious effects and 
00155	jokingly dismiss their need to end or reduce their reliance on 
00156	them.] 
00157	The Evolutionary Basis of Attachment Needs  
00158	The human brain evolved during the Hunter-Gatherer era that 
00159	represents 99.8% of our time on this planet. Children’s 
00160	vulnerability to predators caused them to evolve an intense, 
00161	instinctual fear response to being left alone without protection. 
00162	Fear hard-wired in the child as a healthy response to separation 
00163	from a protective adult, and linked automatically to the fight 
00164	response so that the infant and toddler would automatically cry 
00165	angrily for attention, help, cessation of abandonment - even at the 
00166	briefest loss of contact with parental figures. Beasts of prey only 
00167	needed seconds to snatch away the unprotected child.  
00168	In present time dysfunctional families, many parents disdain 
00169	children for needing their attention. Even the most well-intentioned 
00170	can seriously neglect the child by subscribing to the egregious 20
00171	th 
00172	century ‘wisdom’: “Kids need quality time …not quantity”.  
00173	When children experience long periods of being powerless to 
00174	obtain needed connection with a parent, they become increasingly 
00175	anxious, upset and depressed. Over time their dominant experience 
00176	of self is so replete with emotional pain and so unmanageable that 
00177	that they have to dissociate, act out [aggression against others] or 
00178	act in [aggression against the self] to distract from it. The situation 
00179	of the abandoned child further deteriorates as an extended absence 
00180	of warmth and protection gives rise to the cancerous growth of the 
00181	inner critic as described above. The child projects his hope for 
00182	being accepted onto inner demands of  self-perfection. By the time 
00183	the child is becoming self-reflective, cognitions start to arise that 
00184	sound like this: “I’m so despicable, worthless, unlovable, ugly; 
00185	maybe my parents would love me if I could make myself like those 
00186	perfect kids I see on TV.”  
00187	In this way, the child becomes hyperaware of imperfections and 
00188	strives to become flawless. Eventually she  roots out the ultimate 
00189	flaw – the mortal sin of wanting or asking for her parents’ time or 
00190	energy. Intrinsic to this process is noticing – more and more 
00191	hypervigilantly - how parents turn their back or become angry or 
00192	disgusted whenever she needs anything, whether it be attention, 
00193	listening, interest, or affection.  
00194	Emotional neglect, alone, causes children to abandon themselves, 
00195	and to give up on the formation of a self. They do so to preserve an 
00196	illusion of connection with the parent and to protect themselves 
00197	from the danger of losing that tenuous connection. This typically 
00198	requires a great deal of self-abdication, i.e., the forfeiture of self-
00199	esteem, self-confidence, self-care, self-interest, self-protection. 
00200	Moreover, ever-developing endangerment programs proliferate in 
00201	the critic as the child learns that he cannot ask the dangerous parent 
00202	to protect him from outside world dangers and injustices. His only 
00203	recourse is to become hyperaware and on constant look out for 
00204	things that may go wrong, and the list of such possibilities 
00205	becomes endless, especially when they are graphically illustrated 
00206	and overemphasized on the television. Consciousness eventually 
00207	becomes overwhelmed with the processes of drasticizing and 
00208	catastrophizing– the processes by which the child constantly 
00209	rehearses dreaded and dreadful scenarios in a vain attempt to 
00210	prepare himself for the worst. This is the process by which 
00211	Complex PTSD with its overdeveloped stress and toxic shame 
00212	programs sets in and becomes triggerable by a plethora of  
00213	normally innocuous stimuli. Most notable of these stimuli are other 
00214	people, especially unknown people or people even vaguely 
00215	reminiscent of the parents. Over time, the critic comes to assume 
00216	that other people are dangerous and automatically triggers the 
00217	fight/flight/freeze/fawn response [ See my article: “ The Four F’s: 
00218	A Trauma Typology”] whenever a stranger or unproven other 
00219	comes into view. This process becomes the social phobia that is 
00220	frequently a symptom of complex ptsd.  
00221	Abandonment Stultifies Emotional and Relational Intelligence 
00222	 Emotional intelligence and its cohort, relational intelligence, never 
00223	get to develop, and children never learn that a relationship with a 
00224	healthy person can become an irreplaceable source of comfort and 
00225	enrichment. Moreover, the appropriate management of the normal 
00226	emotions that recurrently arise in significant relationships is never 
00227	modeled for them. Emotional intelligence about the healthy and 
00228	functional aspects of anger, sadness, and fear lies fallow.  
00229	Moreover the receptor sites for receiving love and caring from 
00230	others often lay dormant and undeveloped. Emotionally abandoned 
00231	children often devolve into experiencing all people as dangerous, 
00232	no matter how benign or generous they may in fact be. Anyone can 
00233	automatically  trigger the grown-up child into the deeply grooved 
00234	patterns of perfectionism and endangerment engendered by their 
00235	parents. Love coming their way reverberates threateningly on a 
00236	subliminal level. If, from their perspective, they momentarily 
00237	“trick” someone into seeing them as loveable, they fear that this 
00238	forbidden prize will surely be taken away the minute their social 
00239	perfectionism fails and unmasks some normal flaw or foible. 
00240	 
00241	As with physical abuse, effective work on the wounds of verbal 
00242	and emotional abuse can sometimes open the door to de-
00243	minimizing the awful impact of emotional neglect. I sometimes 
00244	feel the most for my clients who were “only” neglected, because 
00245	without the hard core evidence – the remembering and de-
00246	minimizing of the impact of abuse – they find it extremely difficult 
00247	to connect their non-existent self-esteem, their frequent flashbacks, 
00248	and their recurring reenactments of impoverished relationships,  to 
00249	their childhood emotional abandonment. I repeatedly regret that I 
00250	did not know what I know now about this kind of neglect when I 
00251	wrote my book and over-focused on the role of abuse in childhood 
00252	trauma. It is so hard to convey this to a client whose critic 
00253	minimizes and shames them for their plight by comparing them 
00254	unfavorably to me: “ I didn’t have it anywhere near as bad as you. 
00255	My mother never hit me!”  
00256	How ironic that this typically invokes a  feeling-sense in me that 
00257	by far the worst thing that happened to me, by far, was growing up 
00258	so emotionally abandoned. In fact, it was not until I learned to 
00259	assign the pain of numerous current time emotional flashbacks to 
00260	the abject loneliness of  my childhood, that I was able to work 
00261	effectively on the repetition compulsion that kept me vacillating 
00262	between long periods of isolation and relationships that were never 
00263	safe enough to reveal my whole self. It is important to emphasize 
00264	here that real intimacy, and the healing comfort it alone can 
00265	bestow, depends on showing up in times of  vulnerability – and 
00266	eventually,  and most especially, in the flashbacked-times of 
00267	feeling trapped in the fear, shame and depression of the 
00268	abandonment melange.  
00269	In this vein, I had to painstakingly practice for years showing up in 
00270	my pain and abstaining from my childhood default positions of 
00271	running or hiding or camouflaging with substances whenever I was 
00272	in the grips of  the fear, shame or depression of the abandonment 
00273	melange. How else would I ever have learned that I was loveable 
00274	and acceptable in all aspects of my experience, not just in the 
00275	social perfectionism of my people-pleasing codependence? 
00276	And of course, like most survivors, I was ignorant at first that I 
00277	was experiencing the emotional pain of the abandonment melange; 
00278	how could I help but conceal it? Yet, even after considerable de-
00279	minimization of my childhood abuse/neglect picture, I still 
00280	remained convinced for a long time that everyone but my therapist 
00281	[who in deep flashbacks, I also recurrently distrusted] would find 
00282	me abhorrent if I presented myself authentically in such condition. 
00283	Gratefully, sufficient positive experiences with my therapist 
00284	eventually emboldened me to bring my authentic vulnerability to 
00285	other select and gradually proven relationships, where I found the 
00286	acceptance, safety and support that, previously, I would not have 
00287	even known to wish for.

00289	It is important to note the limitations of the analogy of the onion. 
00290	Effective recovery does typically involve working at various levels 
00291	at the same time. De-minimization is a lifetime process, and 
00292	remembering a crucial instance of being abused or neglected may 
00293	occasionally impact us even more deeply on subsequent 
00294	remembering as we more fully apprehend the hurt of  particularly 
00295	destructive parental betrayals. One such occasion left me reeling 
00296	with the certain knowledge that getting hit felt preferable to being 
00297	abandoned for long hours outside my depressed mother’s locked 
00298	bedroom door. I have known about the latter for quite some time 
00299	now, and yet writing about it brings up some new bitter-sweet 
00300	tears. For me, my ongoing work with the layers of the denial onion 
00301	still sometimes has a bitter-sweet quality to it – bitter because 
00302	abandonment was the worst thing that happened to me, especially 
00303	as it happened over and over again at such a young and normally 
00304	needy age - and sweet because these tears validate the truth of this 
00305	recollection - and sweet, in gratitude, because I do now regularly 
00306	experience good enough love and safety in relationship – and now 
00307	bitter again because I can still emotionally flashback to that bereft 
00308	state of feeling stranded from the comfort of others, even 
00309	occasionally from my wife and son and inner circle-friends – and 
00310	then sweet again because, ongoingly, the frequency, duration and 
00311	intensity of these flashbacks decreases as I increasingly master the 
00312	use of the tools I describe in my article, “Emotional Flashback 
00313	Management.” I am also blessed to see this same progress in 
00314	various of my long term clients who work with this model.

00316	The Neuroplasticity of the Brain 
00317	I am so heartened to know about all the new neuroscience research 
00318	that proves the neuroplasticity of the brain, i.e., that the brain can 
00319	grow and change throughout our life: old self-destructive neural 
00320	pathways can be diminished and new healthier ones grown. [A 
00321	General Theory of Love by Thomas Lewis inspiringly explicates 
00322	this fact]. The critic can indeed literally be shrunk via long-term, 
00323	frequent and dedicated use of the thought-stopping, thought-
00324	substitution and thought-correction practices I describe in my 
00325	articles on the critic. This is especially true when these techniques 
00326	are empowered by the grieving processes I describe in my book, 
00327	The Tao of Fully Feeling, and in an article, “Grieving and 
00328	Complex PTSD”, that I will post on my website around the end of 
00329	2010. 
00330	There is also growing evidence that recovery from Complex PTSD 
00331	is reflected in the narrative a person tells about her life. The degree 
00332	of recovery matches the degree to which a survivor’s story is 
00333	complete, coherent , emotionally congruent and told from a self-
00334	sympathetic perspective. In my experience, deep level recovery is 
00335	often reflected in a narrative that places emotional neglect at the 
00336	core of the understanding of what one has suffered and what one 
00337	continues to deal with. It is a very empowering accomplishment to 
00338	really get the profound significance of childhood emotional neglect 
00339	- to realize in the moment how a flashback into bewilderment, 
00340	panic, toxic shame, helplessness, and hopelessness is an emotional 
00341	reliving of the dominant emotional tone of one’s childhood reality. 
00342	Like nothing else, this can generate self-compassion for one’s 
00343	child-self and one’s present-time self, kick-starting the process of 
00344	resolving any given flashback. This also assuages emotional 
00345	neglect by providing the self with the essential missed childhood 
00346	experience of receiving empathy in painful emotional states instead 
00347	of contempt or abandonment. This, in turn, proves that there has 
00348	been significant deconstruction of the learned, unconscious habit 
00349	of pervasive self-abandonment.
