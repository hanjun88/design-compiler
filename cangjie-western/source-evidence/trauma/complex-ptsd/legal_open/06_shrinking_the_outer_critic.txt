00002	SHRINKING THE OUTER CRITIC IN COMPLEX PTSD 
00003	East Bay Therapist ,  Oct 2009 
00004	By Pete Walker 
00005	               
00006	This article describes childhood trauma’s role in creating an intimacy-destroying, toxic 
00007	outer critic – a counterpart of the self esteem-destroying inner critic described in my 
00008	article: “Shrinking the Inner Critic in Complex PTSD”. The outer critic projects onto 
00009	others the same processes of perfectionism and endangerment that the inner critic uses 
00010	against the self. It perseverates about the unworthiness [imperfection] and 
00011	treacherousness [dangerousness] of others to avoid emotional investment in relationships 
00012	for fear they will replicate early parental betrayals. 
00013	 
00014	The outer critic builds fortresses of isolation whose walls are enumerations of the 
00015	exaggerated shortcomings and potential treacheries of others. In an awful irony, the critic 
00016	attempts to protect us from abandonment by scaring us further into it. If we are ever to 
00017	discover the as yet unknown comfort of soothing connection with others, the critic’s 
00018	dictatorship of the mind must be broken. The critic’s arsenal of intimacy-spoiling 
00019	dynamics must be consciously identified, confronted, suppressed and gradually 
00020	deactivated. Advice about deconstructing fourteen key programs of the critic can be 
00021	found in the above-mentioned article.  
00022	 
00023	The outer critic typically arises most powerfully during emotional flashbacks when it 
00024	transmutes unconscious abandonment pain into an overwhelmingly negative perception 
00025	of people in general and /or of life itself. It obsessively fantasizes, consciously and 
00026	unconsciously, about how people have or could hurt us. Over the years these fantasies 
00027	can expand from scary still-lifes into film clips, and even movies, eventually morphing 
00028	into a veritable video collection of real and imagined betrayals that destroy our capacity 
00029	to be nurtured by human contact. “Don’t trust anyone”, “Proud to be a loner”, “You can 
00030	only depend on yourself”, “Lovers always leave you”, “Kids will break your heart”, 
00031	“Only fools let on what they really think”, “Give them an inch and they’ll take a mile”, 
00032	are titles of video themes survivors may develop in their quest for interpersonal safety. 
00033	These defensive daydreams are analogs of the critic-spawned nightmares that also shore 
00034	up the “safety” work of frightening us into isolation. [Over time, with enough recovery, 
00035	intrusive anti-intimacy reveries become clues that we are actually in a flashback, and that 
00036	we need to invoke our flashback management skills. {See “Managing Emotional 
00037	Flashbacks in Complex PTSD”}]. 
00038	 
00039	 The dynamics of the outer critic are often obscured by minimization and denial. Because 
00040	the critic develops and dominates the psyche so early in childhood, its obsessions and 
00041	“daymares” often fade out of awareness, and become subliminal like the sound of waves 
00042	at the beach…like the sounds of traffic in the city…like th e sound of the critic 
00043	repetitively calling you or someone else a jerk, a loser, an asshole. 
00044	 
00045	Sometimes the outer critic’s penchant for raising false alarms ensnares us with an 
00046	insatiable hunger for listening to the news. When we do not resist this junk food feeding 
00047	of the psyche with news “service” that exults so thoroughly in the negative, we can be 
00048	left floundering in a dreadful hypervigilance. The critic can then work overtime to amass 
00049	irrefutable proof that the world is beyond dangerous, and therefore isolation and minimal 
00050	or superficial relating are our only recourse. At such times any inclination to call a friend 
00051	triggers images of rejection and humiliation before the phone can even be picked up. 
00052	When flashbacks are particularly intense, impulses to venture out may immediately 
00053	trigger fantasies of being verbally harassed or even mugged on the street. 
00054	 
00055	Intimacy and the Outer Critic. Complex PTSD typically includes an attachment 
00056	disorder, which arises from the childhood experience of not having at least one caretaker 
00057	safe enough to go to for comfort or help. When the developing child lacks a supportive 
00058	parental refuge, she never learns that interrelating can soothe and metabolize confusions, 
00059	conflicts and hurts. She also never learns that real intimacy grows out of sharing all of 
00060	one’s experience – the good and the bad, the happy and the sad, the loving and the mad. 
00061	To the degree we are vulnerable and authentic in relationship, to that degree do we 
00062	experience the incomparable healing power of intimacy. However, to the degree that our 
00063	caretakers attack, shame or abandon us for showing vulnerability, to that degree do we 
00064	later avoid the authentic self-expression fundamental to intimacy. Inclinations to 
00065	verbalize feelings, ask for help or reveal one’s struggles are short-circuited by subliminal 
00066	memories of being scorned or attacked for daring to seek our parents’ support. Even 
00067	worse, retaliation fantasies can plague us for hours and days on the occasions we do show 
00068	our vulnerabilities. I once experienced this after being very honest and vulnerable in a job 
00069	interview with a committee of eight. Over the next three insomnia-plagued nights, my 
00070	critic ran non-stop films featuring my interviewers’ [who subsequently hired me] 
00071	contempt about everything I had said, and disgust about all that I had left out.  
00072	 
00073	Passive-Aggressiveness and The Outer Critic  
00074	Children are initially wired to respond angrily to parental abuse or neglect until they learn 
00075	that protesting parental unfairness is the greatest and most punishable crime possible. 
00076	This then renders their anger silent and subliminal where it percolates as an ever 
00077	accumulating sea of resentment, that fuels the critic’s prodigious habits of fault finding 
00078	and seeing danger in everyone. Viewing all relationships through the lenses of parental 
00079	abandonment, the outer critic never lets down its guard. It continuously projects old 
00080	unworked through childhood anger onto others and silently scapegoats them by blowing 
00081	current disappointments out of proportion. It then cites these insignificant transgressions 
00082	as justification for relentless fuming, silent grumbling and long resentful rumination. To 
00083	bastardize Elizabeth Barrett Browning: “How do I find thee lacking? Let me count the 
00084	ways.” 
00085	 
00086	When the displaced blaming of the wrong person becomes habitual, it manifests as 
00087	passive-aggressiveness. Common examples of this are distancing oneself in hurt and 
00088	irritable withdrawal or pushing others away via backhanded compliments, hurtful teasing, 
00089	poor listening and the withholding of positive feedback and appreciation. Chronic 
00090	lateness and poor follow through on comittments can also be an unconscious, passive-
00091	aggressive way of  expressing anger to others. 
00092	 
00093	Vacillating Between Outer and Inner Critic.   Many PTSD survivors flounder in 
00094	caustic judgmentalness, shuffling back and forth between pathologizing others [toxic 
00095	blame] and pathologizing themselves [the toxic shame of the inner critic]. They get stuck 
00096	in endless loops of detailing the relational inadequacies of others, and then of themselves. 
00097	My parents’ twisted version of this boiled down to: “As fucked up as we are, we’re still 
00098	way better than you”. Frieda Fromm-Reichman described this trauma two-step as all-or-
00099	none lurching between the polarities of the grandiose self and the despised self.  When 
00100	we become lost in this process, we miss out on our crucial emotional need to experience a 
00101	sense of belonging, as we lurch between the extremes of too good for others or too rotten 
00102	to be loved. This is the social perfectionism of the Janus-faced critic: others are too 
00103	flawed to love and we are too defective to be lovable. 
00104	 
00105	 Here is a verbal diagram of a typical critic-looping scenario. The need to escape the “in-
00106	danger” feeling that is triggered by socializing or even the thought of it excites the outer 
00107	critic’s judgementalness and subsequent isolation-seeking behavior. Extended withdrawal 
00108	however, reawakens a relational hunger and the impulse to connect. This simultaneously 
00109	reverses the critic from outer to inner mode, which then laundry lists one’s own personal 
00110	inadequacies, which in turn creates self-pitying persecution fantasies, which then 
00111	reinvokes the outer critic mode of inventorying one’s self-righteous resentments of 
00112	others…ad infinitem…ad nauseam - all in the “safe” hiding of silent disengagement. This 
00113	is also a common thread of the fabric of an addiction to worrying. 
00114	 
00115	 It is important to note here that some 4F types [see: “A Trauma Typology: The Four 
00116	F’s”] have different ratios of outer and inner critic engagement and some polarize to one 
00117	or the other. The Freeze [Dissociative] type polarizes to the outer critic blanketly 
00118	denouncing the entire outside world to justify its preference for isolation; the Fight 
00119	[Narcissistic] type polarizes to and speaks from the outer critic to scare away anyone who 
00120	gets too close; the Flight [Obsessive-Compulsive] uses inner critic perfectionistic striving 
00121	to develop performance standards from which the outer critic judges everyone else as 
00122	inferior; and the Fawn [Codependent] type uses inner critic self-hate to self-censor and 
00123	avoid the danger of authentically showing up in relationship. Finally it is not unusual for 
00124	recovering survivors who have significantly shrunk their dominant critic mode to 
00125	experience a reciprocal increase in the virulence of its opposite counterpart, which then 
00126	becomes an opportunity to further shrink the entire agency of the critic. 
00127	 
00128	The Critic as Judge, Jury and Executioner. Not all survivors hide their outer critic. 
00129	Fight typyes and subtypes can take the passive out of passive-aggressive and become 
00130	very aggressive. Such outer critic polarized types often develop a specious belief that 
00131	their subjectively derived standards of correctness are objective truth. At times of intense 
00132	triggering the outer critic uses its distorted detective-lawyer-judge function to build 
00133	hyperbolized cases out of scarce evidence [imagined slights, insignificant pecadillos, 
00134	misread facial expressions, inaccurate “psychic” perceptions] to put potentially intimate 
00135	relationships on trial. In the proceedings, it refuses to admit positive evidence or consider 
00136	extenuating circumstances and is quick to interpret any relational disappointment as a 
00137	deal-canceling, unforgivable betrayal. This is also the process by which jealousy becomes 
00138	toxic and runs riot. 
00139	 
00140	Once the outer critic has become adept at building a case, it ascends to a higher moral 
00141	ground and claims the right to micromanage others. Typically this is rationalized as being 
00142	for the other’s own good, but unconsciously is designed to protect the survivor from any 
00143	reenactment of early parental abuse or neglect. Micromanagement of others can then 
00144	further devolve into treating them like captive audiences, giving them unsolicited 
00145	performance evaluations, making unreasonable demands for improvement, controlling 
00146	their time schedules, social calendars and food and clothing choices, and, in worse case 
00147	scenarios, dramatically acting out jealousy. At its worst, it looks like taking prisoners, not 
00148	making friends. 
00149	 
00150	Overt Scapegoating. Scapegoating is an extreme outer critic process whereby personal 
00151	frustration – typically fuelled by unworked through, childhood abandonment anger - is 
00152	unfairly dumped onto others.  Displacing anger however, not only fails to release or 
00153	resolve old or unrelated hurts, it exacerbates them by destroying others’ capacity to feel 
00154	warmth or appreciation for us. Scapegoating is also typically a reenactment of a parent’s 
00155	abusive role – a parent whose own abandonment depression morphed into a rage that was 
00156	targeted onto a child scapegoat, as the parent made sure that if she felt bad so did 
00157	everyone else. Here is a common example of this type of scapegoating. An individual 
00158	may be absolutely certain that her intense anger about a friend being five minutes late is 
00159	only about his transgression, but with Complex PTSD,  it is often merely the iceberg-tip 
00160	of her unexpressed anger at her parents’ transgressions – in this case, her parents’ chronic 
00161	lateness and failure to show up over and over to meet her normal childhood needs. 
00162	 
00163	Shrinking the Outer Critic through Thought Stopping. Reducing outer critic 
00164	reactivity requires a great deal of mindfulness. This is especially true for trauma types 
00165	who rarely act out their criticalness yet still roil with diatribes against the entire human 
00166	group known as “F***ing People!”. As with inner critic work, the two key fronts for 
00167	critic shrinking are cognitive and emotional. The cognitive work is the demolition and 
00168	rebuilding processes of thought-stopping and thought substitution, respectively. The 
00169	emotional work is grief work - the labor of love that weds the unexpressed childhood 
00170	anger that is healthy self-protection with the uncried childhood tears of self-compassion.  
00171	Let us look first at the cognitive work. Thought stopping is the process of using 
00172	willpower to disidentify from and interrupt toxic thoughts and visualizations. Saying 
00173	“No!” or “Stop!” or “Shut Up!” to drasticizing and perfectionistic mental processes is the 
00174	hammer of self-renovating carpentry. Saying “No!” to the critic not only sets an internal 
00175	boundary against unnatural, anti-self processes, but also acts as a catalyst for outer 
00176	boundary development. [My article, “Shrinking The Inner Critic” elucidates this principle 
00177	more extensively]. 
00178	 
00179	Unfortunately, in early recovery the outer critic often seems to become worse, bigger, and 
00180	stronger the more we notice and challenge it. We may even think we are 
00181	counterproductively stirring it up by daring to resist it. What is really happening however 
00182	is that we are experiencing an erosion of dissociation, which then provides us with a more 
00183	accurate perception of our psyche’s addiction to drasticizing and passing judgment. What 
00184	is also happening is that we are flashing back to how our parents overreacted to our early, 
00185	typically unremembered, protests at their attacks. This can then make us feel too afraid to 
00186	challenge our own thinking. Hence, survivors in early recovery need to invoke the 
00187	instinct of angry self-protection to empower their thought-stopping.  
00188	Successful critic-shrinking requires thousands of spirited skirmishes with the critic. 
00189	Fierce motivation for this work often simultaneously arises when a more accurate picture 
00190	of family of origin life is constructed - especially one that highlights how defenseless the 
00191	child was against being inculcated with the critic. Most trauma survivors were blank 
00192	slates who were brainwashed [intimidated/shamed/abandoned] into accepting the critic as 
00193	their primary identity. Many now need, especially in the early stages of thought-stopping, 
00194	to empower themselves with a healthy rage at this nasty legacy of parental abandonment. 
00195	My son’s birth graced me with an enormous boost in my motivation to practice thought-
00196	stopping. Witnessing the miracle of his ongoing development so greatly increased my 
00197	drive to emotionally invest in him that my outer critic began to work overtime on its 
00198	endangerment programs. It warned me interminably about the danger of my rapidly 
00199	expanding attachment to him. Trying to protect me from devastation should 
00200	untrustworthy life take him away or render him a “bad seed”, it manufactured the most 
00201	dreadful horror movies of accidents, diseases, kidnapping, mental illness, etc. Had I not 
00202	known how to recognize, interpret and refuse to indulge these catastrophizations [and 
00203	sometimes upstage them with outraged disidentification], I’m sure that my capacity to 
00204	bond with and nurture him would have been seriously compromised. 
00205	 
00206	Outer critic entrenchment is also difficult to dislodge because its parlance is normalized, 
00207	and worse, celebrated in our society…esp ecially on TV. In a society where emotional 
00208	abandonment is epidemic, many influential, seemingly healthy adults model a 
00209	communication style that is rife with the judgmentalness, sarcasm, negativity, fear-
00210	mongering and scapegoating that is characteristic of the outer critic. Giving control of our 
00211	social interactions to the outer critic prohibits the cultivation of the vulnerable 
00212	communication that makes intimacy possible. We must renounce unconscious outer critic 
00213	strategies such as: “I will use angry criticism to make you afraid of me, so I can be safe 
00214	from you”; “Why should I bother with people when everyone is so selfish and corrupt” 
00215	[all-or-none thinking]; “I will perfectionistically micromanage you to prevent you from 
00216	betraying or abandoning me”; “ I will rant and rave or leave at the first sign of a lonely 
00217	feeling, because ‘if you really loved me, I would never feel lonely’”. 
00218	Finally, with enough practice and ongoing cultivation of mindfulness, the healthy 
00219	observing ego will become established enough that willpower alone, without anger, will 
00220	often be enough to disidentify from the critic.  
00221	 
00222	Thought Substitution: Supplanting The Outer Critic. Thought substitution is the 
00223	practice of invoking positive thoughts and pictures about others to help erode the critic’s 
00224	intimacy-spoiling habit of picking them apart. I encourage PTSD sufferers to practice the 
00225	“5 positives to 1 negative” rule that John Gottman’s research has shown to be 
00226	characteristic of the communication style of the many intimacy-successful couples he has 
00227	studied. Since the outer critic was spawned in childhood by parental modeling that 
00228	reversed this ratio, many of us are still developmentally arrested in our need to orient our 
00229	psyches towards noticing what’s good, trustworthy and loveable about others and life in 
00230	general. One powerful thought substitution exercise for strengthening this intimacy-
00231	enhancing perspective is to list ten recollections of positive interaction with a given 
00232	friend, as well as ten of her attributes, and ten of her accomplishments. [This same 
00233	technique works well when we self-apply it to help us separate and individuate from our 
00234	inner critic’s negative self-image]. 
00235	 
00236	The most important thought substitution of all is a switch in the perspective of our 
00237	thinking – a broadening of our overall perspective from the critic/superego’s narrow, 
00238	negative focus to the more balanced and accurate focus of the observing ego/Higher Self.  
00239	Perspective substitution resembles the firing of a bad manager or inept coach – one with a 
00240	distorted view that dwells so much on what is wrong that it rarely sees what is right. 
00241	Perspective-substitution can be enhanced with a spiritual practice of Gratitude. Gratitude 
00242	is a type of mindfulness that looks for empirical proof that life is an incomparable gift 
00243	even though it also quite difficult at times. One powerful exercise for cultivating gratitude 
00244	can be done at bedtime by listing at least ten positive happenings of the day. More often 
00245	than not these will not be all-or-none peak experiences but rather basic and simple 
00246	pleasures and appreciations like a neighbor’s hello, a catchy tune, an engaging color, a 
00247	sweet scent, an enjoyable food serving of the day, a new flower in a local garden, a 
00248	satisfying TV show, a feeling of fitness climbing the stairs, soothing words from a 
00249	favorite author, a pleasant encounter with a pet or wildlife. Decades of this practice have 
00250	helped me immeasurably to upgrade the perspective from which I am viewing my life 
00251	and to dethrone the critic from its life-spoiling point of view. Alignment with the healthy 
00252	observing ego provides us with a more balanced and accurate perception of life and over 
00253	time more genuine experiences of feeling that it is good to be alive. This in turn enhances 
00254	our ability to manage flashbacks and decrease their frequency, intensity and duration.

00256	Defueling The Critic through Grieving. Fear is the key emotion that drives the toxic 
00257	endangerment and perfectionism programs of the critic. We therefore need emotional 
00258	tools to manage the fear that runs haywire during a flashback so that we can prevent it 
00259	from morphing into the frightening endangerment cognitions of the critic. Angering and 
00260	crying, the two emotional processes of grieving, are the penultimate tools for 
00261	metabolizing fear before it can be co-opted and deployed by the critic. 
00262	Here is a closer look at the dynamic of grieving out fear. If fear is the temporary death of 
00263	feeling safe, it appears as if children are wired to release fear through angering and 
00264	crying. The newborn baby, mourning the death of living safely inside the mother, utters 
00265	the first of many angry cries to not only call for attention but also to manage her fear. 
00266	Before long however, many dysfunctional parents punish children for emoting. The anger 
00267	of the child gets trapped inside and soon fuels an angry internal looping between inner 
00268	and outer critic, actually exacerbating the fear by creating an increasingly alienated and 
00269	alienating internal environment.  
00270	 
00271	Angrily saying “No!” to the critic, the sabotaging proxy of our dysfunctional caregivers, 
00272	therapeutically externalizes our anger so it is not internalized against us or used 
00273	destructively against our potential intimates. Psychodynamically speaking, angering at 
00274	the critic helps us to work through unresolved transferrential anger that emanates from 
00275	the past and gets destructively displaced onto present relationships. Angering can also 
00276	rescue us from the childlike sense of powerlessness we are flashing back to, further 
00277	serving to antidote fear. Finally, angering also zero sums the critic’s shame supply as it 
00278	reverses Erik Eriksen’s equation: “Shame is blame turned against the self.”  
00279	Crying, the complementary grieving process to anger, is also an irreplaceable tool for 
00280	cutting off the critic’s emotional fuel supply. Tears can release fear before it devolves 
00281	into frightened and frightening thinking. This is especially true when we cry for the 
00282	suffering of our childhood abandonment as well as for the lifetime of unnecessary pain 
00283	that our critic has wrought upon us. Such tears can awaken our developmentally arrested 
00284	sense of self-compassion, which in turn augments our motivation to keep fighting to 
00285	establish internal boundaries against the critic. I have witnessed my own inner and outer 
00286	critic wither into innocuousness hundreds of times after a good cry. On thousands of 
00287	other occasions, I have seen my clients dissolve their shame, fear and self-abandonment 
00288	with the solvent of their tears. 
00289	 
00290	The angering and crying of grieving also encourage and empower the myriad thought 
00291	substitutions needed to affirm and establish a belief in the essential goodness and 
00292	lovability of discriminately chosen others. Griefwork also bolsters us for the long-term, 
00293	frustratingly gradual process of reeducating the psyche to make it more intimacy friendly 
00294	[shrinking the outer critic] and more user-friendly [diminishing the inner critic]. 
00295	Extensive guidelines and encouragements for navigating the long, daunting, three-steps-
00296	forward and two-steps-backward process of grieving the losses of childhood and 
00297	effectively shrinking the critic are contained in my book, The Tao of  Fully Feeling, 
00298	Harvesting [Self] Forgiveness out of [Self] Blame.
