00014	This article explores the role of grieving in treating childhood trauma and Complex 
00015	Posttraumatic Stress Disorder. Insight, as crucially important as it is, is never enough in 
00016	recovery. No amount of intention or epiphany can bypass one’s need to learn to lovingly 
00017	care for himself when he is suffering from the emotional flashbacks of C-PTSD. 
00018	Emotional flashbacks are regressions that take the survivor back to the excruciating states 
00019	of fear, humiliation, abandonment, helplessness and hopelessness that he nearly drowned 
00020	in during childhood. Grieving is an irreplaceable tool for metabolizing and resolving the 
00021	overwhelming feelings that arise during emotional flashbacks. 
00022	Grieving aids the survivor immeasurably to work through the innumerable death-like 
00023	experiences of being lost and trapped in emotional flashbacks. Grieving also supports 
00024	recovery from the many painful, death-like losses caused by childhood traumatization. 
00025	Recoverees need to grieve the death of safety and belonging in their own childhoods – 
00026	the death of their early attachment needs. They need to mourn the myriad heartbreaks of 
00027	their frustrated attempts to win approval and affection from their parents.  
00028	As the grieving process therapeutically evolves, survivors typically uncover a great deal 
00029	of unresolved grief about the deadening absence of the nurturance they needed to develop 
00030	and thrive. Children will only flourish if the following types of needs are consistently 
00031	met: 1. Physical needs for affection and protection; 2. Emotional needs for caring, regard 
00032	and interest; 3. Spiritual needs for recognition of their worth and basic goodness; 4. 
00033	Verbal needs for welcoming inquiry, positive feedback, and multidimensional 
00034	conversation.
00035	1
00036	It is difficult to become motivated to grieve losses that occurred so long ago. Many of 
00037	these losses seem so nebulous that trying to embrace grieving is a bit like trying to 
00038	embrace dental work. Who wants to go to the dentist? But who doesn’t go once the 
00039	toothache becomes acute. Soul ache is considerably harder to assign to the losses of 
00040	childhood, yet those who take the grieving journey described below come to know 
00041	unquestionably that the core of their soul ache and psychological suffering is in the 
00042	unworked through losses of growing up with abandoning parents. These losses must be 
00043	grieved until the individual really get that her parents were not her allies. She needs to 
00044	grieve until she stops blaming herself for their abuse and/or neglect…until she fully 
00045	realizes that their execrable parenting caused her posttraumatic stress. She needs to grieve 
00046	 
00047	                                                 
00048	 
00049	until she understands how her learned habit of automatic self-abandonment is a 
00050	reenactment of their abject failure to be there for her. 
00051	Mourning these awful realities can then empower her efforts to develop a 
00052	multidimensional practice of self-care. As she grieves more efficaciously, her capacity 
00053	for self-compassion and self-protection grows, and her psyche becomes increasingly user 
00054	friendly 
00055	Grieving Ameliorates Flashbacks  
00056	“Pain is excess energy crying out for release.” – Gerald Heard 
00057	Grieving sometimes seems sacramental to me in its ability to move me out of the 
00058	abandonment melange. The abandonment melange is the extremely painful and upsetting 
00059	amalgam of fear, shame and depression that is at the emotional core of most flashbacks. 
00060	A survivor can learn to grieve himself out of fear - the death of feeling safe. He can learn 
00061	to grieve himself out of shame - the death of feeling worthy. He can learn to grieve 
00062	himself out of depression - the death of feeling fully alive.  
00063	With sufficient grieving, the individual gets that she was innocent and eminently 
00064	loveable. As she mourns the bad luck of not being born to loving parents, she can find 
00065	within herself a fierce, unshakeable self-allegiance. She can become ready, willing and 
00066	able to be there for herself no matter what she is experiencing - internally or externally.  
00067	Inner Critic Hindrances to Grieving 
00068	Let us first look at the greatest hindrance to effective grieving: the Inner Critic. When the 
00069	critic is especially toxic, grieving may be counterproductive and contraindicated in early 
00070	recovery. Those who were repeatedly pathologized and punished for emoting in 
00071	childhood may experience grieving as exacerbating their flashbacks rather than relieving 
00072	them. [My article, “Shrinking The Inner Critic,” identifies 13 common inner critic 
00073	programs. These programs generally constellate around self-hate engendering demands 
00074	for perfection and panic-inducing misperceptions of endangerment. This article also 
00075	describe 13 sets of thought corrections to deconstruct these programs.] 
00076	I have worked with numerous clients whose tears immediately triggered them into toxic 
00077	shame. Their own potentially soothing tears elicited terrible self-attacks: “I’m so pathetic! 
00078	No wonder nobody can stand me!.” “God, I’m so unlovable when I snivel like this!” “I 
00079	fuck up, and then make myself more of a loser by whining about it!” “What good is 
00080	crying for yourself – it only makes you weaker!”  
00081	This latter response is particularly ironic, for once grieving is protected from the critic, 
00082	nothing can restore the individual’s inner strength and coping capacity like a good cry. I 
00083	have defused active suicidality on dozens of occasions by simply eliciting the client’s 
00084	tears. On thousands of occasions, I have led clients out of flashbacks by helping them cry. 
00085	Critic management is often the primary work of early stage recovery. Once the critic has 
00086	been sufficiently diminished and once thought correction techniques have made the 
00087	psyche more user-friendly, the individual begins to tap into grief’s sweet relief-granting 
00088	potential. She learns to grieve in a way that promotes and enhances compassion for the 
00089	abandoned child she was and for the survivor she is today – still frequently struggling in 
00090	the throes of flashbacks. 
00091	 
00092	Defueling The Critic through Grieving. 
00093	Fear drives the toxic inner critic. The critic feeds off fear and flashes the survivor back to 
00094	the frightening times of childhood. He gets stuck seeing himself only through his parents’ 
00095	contemptuous, intimidating or rejecting eyes. He mirrors them and scornfully mocks 
00096	himself as “defective”, “ugly”, “unlovable”. He scares himself with endangerment 
00097	scenarios and abhors himself for insignificant imperfections. 
00098	Because fear is a  core emotional experience, emotional tools are needed to manage the 
00099	fright that runs haywire during a flashback. Healthy angering and crying can short-circuit 
00100	fear from morphing into the flashback-triggering cognitions of the critic. I have seen 
00101	grieving bring the critic’s devastating programs of drasticizing and catastrophizing to a 
00102	screeching halt on thousands of occasions. 
00103	It appears to me that children are wired to release fear through angering and crying. The 
00104	newborn baby, mourning the death of living safely and fully contained inside the mother, 
00105	utters the first of many angry cries not only to call for nurturance and attention, but also 
00106	to release her fear. In the dysfunctional family however, the traumatizing parent soon 
00107	punishes the child for emoting. The child becomes afraid and ashamed of her own tears 
00108	and anger. Tears get shut off and anger gets trapped inside and is eventually turned 
00109	against the self as self-attack, self-hate, self-disgust, and self-rejection. Self-hate is the 
00110	most grievous reenactment of parental abandonment. 
00111	Over time, anger also becomes fuel for the critic and actually exacerbates fear by creating 
00112	an increasingly dangerous internal environment. Anything the survivor says, thinks, feels, 
00113	imagines or wishes for is subjected to an intimidating inner attack by the critic, which 
00114	eventually morphs into a first person voice: “Why did I ask such a stupid question?” 
00115	“Could I have had an uglier expression on my face?” “Who am I kidding? How could an 
00116	undeserving loser like me wish for love?” “No wonder I feel like shit; I am a piece of 
00117	shit!” Recovery is enhanced immeasurably by co-opting this anger from the critic and 
00118	using it for self-protection, and for the work of distancing from and shrinking the critic. 
00119	 
00120	Let us now explore how the four processes of grieving: Angering, Crying, Verbally 
00121	Ventilating and Feeling are so central to ameliorating Complex PTSD. 
00122	 
00123	1. Angering Diminishes Fear and Shame  
00124	Angering is the grieving technique of aggressively complaining about current or past 
00125	losses and injustices. Survivors  need to anger - sometime rage - about the intimidation, 
00126	humiliation or neglect that was passed off to them as nurturance in their childhoods. As 
00127	they become adept at grieving, they anger out their healthy resentment at their family’s 
00128	pervasive lack of safety – at the ten thousand betrayals of no-one-to-go-to for guidance or 
00129	protection, no one to appeal to for fairness or appreciative recognition of their 
00130	developmental achievements. [My book, The Tao of Fully Feeling, Harvesting 
00131	Forgiveness Out Of Blame, outlines a safe process for angering out childhood pain in a 
00132	way that does not hurt the individual or anyone else.]  
00133	Angering is therapeutic when the survivor rails against childhood trauma, and especially 
00134	when she rails against its living continuance in the self-hate processes of the critic. 
00135	Angrily saying “No!” or “Shut Up!” to the critic, the proxy of her parents, externalizes 
00136	the anger. It stops her from turning her anger against herself, and allows her to revive the 
00137	lost instinct of defending herself against unjust attack. Moreover, it rescues her from 
00138	toxic shame, as it reverses Erik Eriksen’s famous equation: “Shame is blame turned 
00139	against the self.” Angering redirects blame where it belongs. 
00140	Angering can be done alone or in the presence of a validating witness, such as a trusted 
00141	friend or therapist. Over time the vast majority of angering needs to be done silently in 
00142	the privacy of one’s own psyche, as one learns to recognize and respond to inner critic 
00143	attack with self-protective, anger-empowered thought-stopping. 
00144	Many survivors are so identified with the critic, that it becomes their whole identity. Such 
00145	survivors typically need to focus on fighting off the critic until they have established the 
00146	healthy egoic function of self-protection. Until then, they typically derive little benefit 
00147	from practicing the more subtle techniques of critic-management  that are practiced in 
00148	many Psychodynamic and Mindfulness approaches. Embracing Your Inner Critic by 
00149	Stone & Stone describes many of these techniques which can be helpful in later recovery, 
00150	once the virulent, totalitarian voice of the inner critic has been sufficiently quelled. 
00151	Angering also serves to rescue the individual from the childlike sense of powerlessness 
00152	he is flashing back to. It reminds him that he inhabits an adult body and has greater 
00153	capacity for self-protection than he did as a child. As such, angering serves to antidote or 
00154	reduce fear. It reawakens and nurtures the instinct of self-protection. With practice it 
00155	increasingly builds a sense of both outer and inner boundaries – safety from the bullying 
00156	of others and safety from the most damaging bully of all – the inner critic. 
00157	Finally, angering can also empower the myriad thought corrections and substitutions 
00158	needed to establish the survivor’s belief in her own essential goodness and in the 
00159	lovability of discriminately chosen others. Angering bolsters her for the long-term, 
00160	gradual process of wrestling her self-image away from the critic and reeducating the 
00161	psyche to make it both user- and intimacy-friendly.  
00162	Angering helps deconstruct repetition compulsion 
00163	Until the survivor reconnects with his instinctual anger about the abusive and neglectful 
00164	behaviors that his parents forbade him to complain about, he risks blindly accepting 
00165	others’ reenactments of these behaviors. Here is an example of this. A meek, visibly 
00166	fearful client of mine suffered devastating sexual seductions by trusted male figures on 
00167	three occasions in her adult life. Over time we traced these back to a childhood betrayal 
00168	by a trusted uncle, the only seemingly kind caretaker of her childhood. He preyed on her 
00169	loneliness and parental abandonment and gradually took seemingly appropriate physical 
00170	affection, one increment at a time, into contact that became increasingly sexual. Since her 
00171	healthy angry self-protective instinct and ability too say “no” was extinguished by the 
00172	time she was in pre-school, she was unable to protest his sexual violations. On 
00173	subsequent occasions in her life, a minister, a doctor and then a therapist exploited her via 
00174	a reenactment of this original scenario. She was so lost in flashback all three times that 
00175	she did not protest their exploitive betrayals, but only knew how to turn her anger inward 
00176	and blame and shame herself. 
00177	 Eventually during our work together, she was able to engage in the angering process of 
00178	grieving. After about six months of my witnessing and validating her angering at her 
00179	various perpetrators, she came in one week glowing with pride about her success in 
00180	stopping an office predator who was in the early stages of a similar seduction. This was 
00181	the stage of seemingly friendly touching, unwanted pats on the back that gradually 
00182	escalated into mild sexual innuendo, lingering touches on her hand and  then her forearm. 
00183	These were the first inappropriate advances that she had never been able to protest with 
00184	her previous abusers. She was thrilled  - in awe of herself – that she was able to say, in 
00185	the presence of another worker no less: “Please don’t touch me. I don’t like it when you 
00186	touch me and I don’t want you to touch me anymore”. The seduction was immediately 
00187	terminated.  
00188	2. Crying: The Penultimate Soother 
00189	In grieving, crying is the yin complementary process to the yang process of angering. 
00190	When we are hurt, we instinctively feel sad as well as mad. The newborn child, hurt by 
00191	the loss of the perfect security of the womb, howls an angry cry. 
00192	As we learn to grieve effectively, we allow ourselves to mourn about the dearth of 
00193	positive parental attention in our childhoods. We feel sorrow about the horrible reality 
00194	that parental attention was typically negative and dangerous. And we cry in self-
00195	compassion that we were rarely or never appreciated and reflected as special, worthy, 
00196	easy to love. 
00197	Crying is often the only process that will resolve a flashback. Unashamed crying appears 
00198	to cut off the critic’s fuel supply of fear before it devolves into frightened and frightening 
00199	thinking. This is especially true when we cry for the suffering of our childhood 
00200	abandonment, as well as for the lifetime of unnecessary alienation and pain that our critic 
00201	has wrought upon us.  
00202	Moreover, unabashed tears stimulate the relaxation response of the parasympathetic 
00203	nervous system, and counterbalance the excessive sympathetic nervous system 
00204	hyperarousal we experience in a flashback.  
00205	Crying and self-compassion. 
00206	When we greet our own tears with self-acceptance, crying awakens our developmentally 
00207	arrested sense of self-compassion. Once we establish self-compassion through consistent 
00208	and repeated practice, it becomes the cornerstone of an increasing sense of self-esteem. 
00209	When an attitude of self-compassion becomes habitual, it can instantly antidote the self-
00210	abandonment that so characterizes a flashback. Moreover self-compassion creates a 
00211	foundation from which we can build authentic, intimacy-enhancing compassion for 
00212	others. Our ability to be there for an intimate depends on our capacity to practice 
00213	unwavering allegiance to ourselves. 
00214	Self-compassion also naturally sustains our motivation for the long, repetitive work of 
00215	fortifying our internal boundaries against the critic. I have witnessed my own inner and 
00216	outer critic wither into innocuousness hundreds of times after a good cry. On thousands 
00217	of other occasions, I have seen my clients dissolve their fear, shame and self-
00218	abandonment with the solvent of their tears. 
00219	A new 35 year-old client, self-identified as a macho-loner, came to see me fearing that 
00220	three days of sleeplessness were about to drive him over the edge. His thick persona of 
00221	unflappability was inaccessible to him for the first time that he could remember. “I can’t 
00222	believe it. I can’t believe it. I’ve become a total fruitcake. I’ve been holed up in my 
00223	apartment for three days. I can’t bear for anybody to see me. I can’t even take out the 
00224	garbage. I always secretly knew that down deep I was a disgusting wimp.”  
00225	He went on to describe how he had recently witnessed his best friend – his black 
00226	Labrador dog, George - get crushed by a passing car when he raced across the street in 
00227	pursuit of a squirrel. It was the worst thing he had ever seen, and he was plagued and 
00228	obsessed with the images of it, especially in bed at night as sleep eluded him. 
00229	 He had been awake for the last 96 hours, and he was sure that he was going insane. 
00230	Halfway through the session I asked him what he missed about George, and soon the 
00231	floodgates opened. He sobbed and sobbed for about ten minutes, and came out of it 
00232	looking like a new man. He enthusiastically said: “ I haven’t cried since I was six. I can’t 
00233	believe how light and relaxed and peaceful I feel. This is better than beer or any downer 
00234	I’ve ever taken. I can tell I am going to be OK now. I’m seeing George as he was when 
00235	he was alive – not as that horrible mangled mess on the street.” 
00236	Crying and angering together 
00237	Crying and angering are the two key emotional tools that we have for releasing the pain 
00238	of the abandonment melange. It is important to note here that most of us suffer an 
00239	acculturation process that dictates that men should only emote through angering and 
00240	women through crying. From an early age boys are punished and shamed for crying 
00241	while girls are treated similarly for angering. Hence boys become men who learn to 
00242	automatically externalize the pain of their fear, shame and depression through anger 
00243	while women do the same with tears. Such men can only get pissed off when they are 
00244	scared, humiliated or feeling down, while such women can only cry out these feelings. 
00245	This typically results in a very incomplete release. Half of the hurt release process is 
00246	stymied. It is as if the motion of emotion is arrested; emotion deteriorates into a stagnant 
00247	and lingering mood as anger degenerates into bitter resentment and sadness into 
00248	unregenerate self-pity. Without access to tears, the “angerer” seethes and without access 
00249	to anger, the crier whines. Without complete emoting of the hurt, the survivor – or 
00250	anyone for that matter – often becomes stuck in moodiness … in painful internal 
00251	vacillations between angry resentment and melancholic self-pity. 
00252	How this contrasts with the healthy grieving of my six-year old son, who has to 
00253	periodically grieve my gradual thwarting of his diminishing narcissistic entitlement. As 
00254	he does, he bawls at each new age-appropriate societal rule he has to accept. He howled 
00255	this afternoon about the “unfairness” of having to do his homework after school before he 
00256	could play some more. As we climbed the 37 stairs to our home, he punctuated his crying 
00257	with a flurry of angry condemnations: “I don’t like you daddy. You’re not fair. I’m not 
00258	going to be your friend”. I am grateful for this healthy grieving and still somewhat 
00259	amazed that I can so calmly “hold” it as I help him metabolize this latest loss of early 
00260	childhood narcissistic privilege. By the time we get to the top of the stairs he opens the 
00261	door and genuinely peals out a guileless laugh: “Daddy, look! Picachu feel off the table. 
00262	Daddy can we play Pokemon after I practice writing my letters.” Grieving has almost 
00263	instantly delivered him from painful loss into eager apprehension of what’s fun about life 
00264	and what there is to look forward to. How frequently, I see my clients reemerge into 
00265	remembering their real, present-time resourcefulness once they have grieved through a 
00266	painful flashback.   
00267	When we can both anger and cry while re-experiencing our early abandonment in a 
00268	flashback, we can obtain a more complete release from the abandonment melange. Each 
00269	survivor does well to assess whether his angering or crying is blocked or stultified, and to 
00270	then work at recovering it. 
00271	There are of course numerous men and women who are reverse examples of the gender 
00272	polarization mentioned above. Moreover, there are many survivors in whom both 
00273	angering and crying are blocked. They also benefit inordinately from rescuing the healing 
00274	balm of both anger and tears from the stultifying prohibitions of the critic. 
00275	3. Verbal Ventilation  
00276	Verbal Ventilation is the third process of grieving. It helps resolve emotional flashbacks. 
00277	Verbal ventilation is speaking or writing in a manner that airs out and releases painful 
00278	feelings. When we let our words spring from what we feel, language is imbued with 
00279	emotion, and pain can be released through what we say, think or write. As our grieving 
00280	proficiency increases, we can verbally ventilate about our losses, using shame-dissolving 
00281	language to tell the story of how we were unfairly deprived of our birthright to be 
00282	welcomed into a family that cherished us.  
00283	My favorite technique to enhance verbal ventilation is to encourage the client to focus on 
00284	his feelings, his painful imagery or his bodily sensations, and then to talk in an 
00285	uncensored manner about whatever comes to mind. 
00286	Verbal Ventilation is therapeutic to the degree that a person’s words are colored by and 
00287	descriptive of the anger, sadness, fear, shame and/or depression she feels. Ventilation that 
00288	is liberally punctuated with actual crying or angering is especially powerful.  
00289	Verbal ventilation is a tool that can remediate brain changes that are caused by Complex 
00290	PTSD. Susan Vaughan’s MRI research [The Talking Cure] demonstrates that emotional 
00291	flashbacks create a condition whereby the emotionally oriented right brain becomes over-
00292	activated, and the thinking-oriented left-brain becomes under-activated. This hemispheric 
00293	polarization corresponds with an overwhelming reemergence of childhood pain 
00294	[emotionally remembered in the memory-oriented right brain], and the loss of access to 
00295	the higher cognitive functions of the left brain. This temporary loss of left brain 
00296	functioning explains why it is so hard for a survivor to realize that she is only flashing 
00297	back and not currently stranded in the danger, helplessness and hopelessness of the past. 
00298	Verbal ventilation, at its most potent, is the therapeutic process of bringing left brain 
00299	cognition to intense right brain emotional activation. It fosters the recoveree’s ability to 
00300	put words to feelings, and ultimately to accurately interpret and communicate about his 
00301	various feeling states. When this process is repeated sufficiently, new neural pathways 
00302	grow that allow the left and right brain to work together so that the individual can 
00303	actually think and feel at the same time. He heals a crucial developmental arrest and 
00304	learns to think about feeling states in a way that creates healthy, helpful and appropriate 
00305	responses to feelings - responses that are respectful to him and to whomever the feelings 
00306	arise with. [In his book, Emotional Intelligence, Daniel Goleman writes that this is the 
00307	core trait of emotional intelligence]. 
00308	With continued practice, verbal ventilation coordinates the left and right hemispheres and 
00309	when the right brain is hyper-activated in flashback, the left-brain is also fully engaged 
00310	[as can be seen in an MRI]. With the left brain back on line, the survivor can then initiate 
00311	the various tasks that are helpful in successfully resolving a flashback. [See my article on 
00312	“ Managing Emotional Flashbacks”.] 
00313	As with angering and crying, verbal ventilation is only effective when it is liberated from 
00314	the critic’s control. In early recovery, verbal ventilation can easily shift into verbal self-
00315	flagellation. Verbally ventilating from the self-attacking or drasticizing perspective of the 
00316	critic is rarely effective grieving. Instead it typically triggers or intensifies flashbacks, 
00317	which in turn frequently generates self- and intimacy-injuring behavior. 
00318	Many survivors in early recovery are unable to notice their unconscious shifts into over-
00319	identifying with the critic’s point of view. When this is the case, they usually need the 
00320	help of a therapist or sufficiently recovered intimate to help them recognize and 
00321	neutralize the critic.  
00322	Verbal Ventilation is an especially transformative grieving process. It not only promotes 
00323	the same type of fear-release and shame-dissolution as angering and crying, but also 
00324	helps expose the manifold guises of the critic’s self-attack and fear-arousal. Verbal 
00325	ventilation also helps us to identify and communicate about needs that have long been 
00326	unmet because of  our childhood abandonment. This in turn promotes the growth of the 
00327	healthy ego, and most especially its key functions of self-compassion and self-protection. 
00328	Dissociation deadens verbal ventilation 
00329	It is important to differentiate verbal ventilation from dissociative flights of fantasy or 
00330	obsessive bouts of unproductive worrying. Dissociation is a defense that children  
00331	develop to distract mad protect themselves from the overwhelming pain of their 
00332	abandonment. They have to dissociate because they are not able, as unsupported children, 
00333	to effectively grieve or even allow their pain into awareness. 
00334	 There are two common types of dissociation in Complex PTSD: right-brain dissociation 
00335	and left-brain dissociation.  
00336	Right brain dissociation can be seen as classical dissociation, and as the defense most 
00337	common to freeze types [see my article on “The Four F’s: A Trauma Typology”.] It is the 
00338	right brain process of numbing out against intense feeling or incessant inner critic attack. 
00339	Dissociation is a process of distraction…of getting lost in fantasy, fogginess, TV, 
00340	tiredness or sleep.  
00341	Verbally reporting from a place of self-distraction is in fact the opposite of verbal 
00342	ventilation. Getting lost in daydream-like descriptions of improbable salvation fantasies 
00343	or in the recounting of long elaborate dreams devoid of emotional content and serious 
00344	introspection are examples of this. 
00345	Left-brain dissociation is obsessiveness. Commonly, this ranges in severity from 
00346	repetitively cycling through a laundry list of worries to panicky drasticizing and 
00347	catastrophizing. An obsessive defense against internal pain strands us in unhelpful 
00348	perseverations about issues that are minimally related or unrelated to the true nature of 
00349	our suffering. Here is an example of this. I can complain incessantly about the bad 
00350	weather or the unfairness of the rising cost of movie tickets to distract myself from the 
00351	fact that my partner’s constructive feedback is continuously flashing me back into the 
00352	fear and shame I felt when my mother verbally abused me at the kitchen table.   
00353	Left-brain dissociation can also be a process of trivialization – avoiding upsetting inner 
00354	experience by over-focusing on superficial concerns. Becoming overly preoccupied with 
00355	sports statistics or the lives of Hollywood celebrities are common examples of this; this 
00356	is, of course, not to deny that such interests are in themselves worthwhile when they are 
00357	engaged non-defensively and in moderation. 
00358	Finally, left brain dissociation can also be seen in intellectualization. This is what the 
00359	novelist, Ian McEwan called the “high-walled fortress of focused thinking”. Some 
00360	survivors over-rely on rationalization and lofty dialogue to protect them from the 
00361	potentially messy and painful world of feeling. Even the highest levels of creative 
00362	thinking deteriorate into an obsessive defense when they are excessively engaged. 
00363	Verbal ventilation heals abandonment 
00364	When we allow ourselves to share what is emotionally important to us – and this applies 
00365	to concerns that excite and please us as well as those that frighten, hurt or depress us – we 
00366	can learn to connect with others in an authentic, meaningful and healing way. One of the 
00367	most detrimental consequences of our childhood abandonment is that it forced us to 
00368	habitually hide and mute our selves. Many survivors have come to believe that their 
00369	authentic and vulnerable self-expression is as repugnant or boring to others as it was to 
00370	their parents.  
00371	We must repudiate and overcome this damaging legacy of the past. Verbal ventilation is a 
00372	key process in establishing healthy attachment, as key to children as the tender touch, 
00373	soothing voice, and welcoming facial expressions are to infants and toddlers. When we 
00374	practice this form of emotionally based communication with a safe enough other, we 
00375	repair the damage of not having had this need met in childhood. This in turn opens up the 
00376	possibility of finally attaining the verbal-emotional intimacy that is an essential lifelong 
00377	need for all human beings. 
00378	Unfortunately, committing to this type of practice is much harder than it may sound. 
00379	Authentic and vulnerable sharing can be extremely triggering, and can easily flash the 
00380	survivor back to experiences of being attacked, shamed or abandoned by parents hostile 
00381	or indifferent to such self-disclosure. Therapy, individual or group, may be necessary to 
00382	overcome and work through these huge obstacles to revitalizing our self-expression. 
00383	Verbal ventilation and intimacy  
00384	I believe that reciprocal verbal ventilation is the key process of intimacy in adult 
00385	relationships. Sufficient practice with a safe enough other can eventually open the 
00386	survivor to experiences of comforting and restorative connection with others – 
00387	experiences that can be more alleviating of loneliness than he would ever have thought 
00388	possible. As deep and meaningful connection with another gradually becomes more 
00389	available and frequent, he will increasingly experience amelioration of the frightful, 
00390	shaming and deadening aspects of the abandonment depression. Moreover, he will learn 
00391	the emotional communication skills that help him to process and to healthily integrate the 
00392	difficult emotional experiences [disappointment, disagreement, disaffection] that intimate 
00393	relationships normally and periodically undergo. 
00394	Finally, it is important to note here that there are some bodywork approaches to trauma 
00395	that denigrate the efficacy of talk therapy, and in extreme cases decree that this all 
00396	important process be bypassed and done away with. While somatic work of some kind is 
00397	often essential to our recovering process, it alone cannot remediate the toxic critic or 
00398	resuscitate the instincts of self-compassion and self-protection. A great deal of cognitive 
00399	work, especially that which is empowered by verbal ventilation is fundamental to 
00400	significant recovery from complex ptsd. 
00401	 With that said, we will move on to Feeling, the fourth grieving process – a process that is 
00402	a type of body work, a way of focusing on somatic experience that enables us to reclaim 
00403	our ability to experience full, relaxed and vital inhabitancy of our bodies. 
00404	4. Feeling and Passively Working Through Grief  
00405	“Feeling is the antithesis of pain…the more pain one feels, the less pain one suffers”  
00406	                                                                                                                     –  Arthur Janov 
00407	Ongoing engagement in the active grieving processes of angering, crying and verbal 
00408	ventilation can help us to discover the fourth grieving process: Feeling. Feeling is a 
00409	subtler, passive process. It is best illustrated by contrasting the two concepts “emote” and 
00410	“feel”. Emoting is when we cry or anger out the energy of an inner emotional experience. 
00411	Feeling, on the other hand, is the passive process of non-reactively staying present to 
00412	internal emotional experience. In complex ptsd recovery, feeling is surrendering to the 
00413	internal experiences of our childhood pain without judging or resisting them, and without 
00414	emoting them out. 
00415	Feeling is a kinesthetic rather than a cognitive experience. It is the process of bringing 
00416	one’s awareness out of thinking and into the internal emotions, energetic states and 
00417	sensations of the body. 
00418	As a grieving process, feeling involves consciously reversing the learned survival 
00419	mechanism of clamping down on pain to banish it from awareness.  
00420	Feeling “occurs” when we bring the focus of our consciousness into an emotionally or 
00421	physically painful state, and when we surrender to this experience without resistance. 
00422	When we relax acceptingly into a feeling, we can learn to gently absorb it into our 
00423	experience. This is similar to healthy food digestion, in which a relaxed alimentary canal 
00424	allows us to effectively assimilate nutrients.  
00425	However, when we suppress or repress our feelings, our bodies typically armor and 
00426	tighten all over, especially along the alimentary canal. I believe this latter type of 
00427	tightening also creates the types of digestive problems that frequently co-occur with 
00428	complex ptsd. Diarrhea, for instance, can sometimes be an emotional-physical response 
00429	to intense danger… a fear -induced triggering of the sympathetic nervous system that 
00430	triggers an immediate evacuation of the bowels. Nausea can operate similarly.  
00431	The emotional-physical connection 
00432	It is important to note here that there is often a close relationship between emotion and 
00433	physical sensation. Physical sensations in the body often co-occur with feelings. 
00434	Moreover, sensations of tightness and tension can develop as a defense against feelings. 
00435	As unexpressed feelings accumulate, a greater degree of muscular tension is necessary to 
00436	keep them in check.  
00437	A child who is repeatedly punished for emoting learns to be afraid of inner emotional 
00438	experience and tightens [armors] the musculature of her body in an effort to hold feelings 
00439	in and to banish them from awareness. Holding one’s breath is a further manifestation of 
00440	armoring and an especially common way of keeping feelings at bay, as breathing 
00441	naturally brings awareness down to the feeling level. Deep breathing also expands and 
00442	stretches various visceral muscles in ways that sometimes release feelings and leads to 
00443	emoting. I know of several survivors who were so afraid of exposing their feelings to 
00444	their contemptuous parents that they fainted from resolutely holding their breath. 
00445	[Rebirthing and Reichian therapy employ breathing techniques to help free stuck 
00446	emotions]. 
00447	The technique of focusing one’s awareness on physical sensations in the body can help us 
00448	become more proficient at the practice of “feeling”. With enough practice, paying 
00449	attention to tightness in the face, throat, heart or belly area brings feelings into awareness, 
00450	where they can be simply felt through. 
00451	The passive grieving process of feeling through pain perfectly complements the active 
00452	processes of grieving. We benefit immeasurably from becoming equally facile with 
00453	emoting out our anger, sadness, fear, shame and depression, as with simply staying fully 
00454	present to these feelings without trying to change them. Furthermore, feeling also 
00455	expands our capacity to bring the emotions into awareness that need to be grieved out 
00456	through active, cathartic emoting. Thus, grieving is especially profound when we can 
00457	fluidly shift between feeling and emoting. Sometimes we will only need to fully feel and 
00458	accept the sensations of our pain, and sometimes we will want to verbally ventilate about 
00459	them within a matrix of having full permission to punctuate our words with angering and 
00460	tears. 
00461	Learning to feel 
00462	As a survivor becomes more adept at angering and crying, fear of his feelings will 
00463	decrease, and opportunities to learn to simply feel will present themselves. He can engage 
00464	these opportunities by passively noticing and attuning to the more subtle sensations of his 
00465	anger and sadness. Over time this practice will build his ability to stay feelingly and 
00466	passively present to the sensations of fear, shame and depression. In early stages, this 
00467	awareness will often morph into the need to actively grieve out the fear, shame and 
00468	depression, but eventually these feelings will also be digested and worked through purely 
00469	with the solvent of awareness.  
00470	It is important to note here that anxiety, which is often fear just below the level of 
00471	awareness, can often be felt through passively. My article, “Managing The Abandonment 
00472	Depression”, describes this practice at much greater length. 
00473	An exercise in feeling 
00474	Here is an exercise to help you enhance your ability to feel and grieve through pain. 
00475	Visualize yourself as time-traveling back to a place in the past when you felt especially 
00476	abandoned. See your adult self taking your abandoned child onto your lap and comforting 
00477	her in various painful emotional states or situations. You can comfort her verbally: “I feel 
00478	such sorrow that you were so abandoned and that you felt so alone so much of the time. I 
00479	love you even more when you are stuck in this abandonment pain – especially because 
00480	you had to endure it for so long with no one to comfort you. That shouldn’t have 
00481	happened to you. It shouldn’t happen to any child. Let me comfort and hold you. You 
00482	don’t have to rush to get over it. It is not your fault. You didn’t cause it and you’re not to 
00483	blame. You don’t have to do anything. Let me just hold you. Take you’re time. I love you 
00484	always and care about you no matter what.” 
00485	 I highly recommend practicing this even if it feels inauthentic, and even if it requires a 
00486	great deal of fending off your critic. Keep practicing and eventually, you will have a 
00487	genuine experience of feeling self-compassion for that traumatized child you were, and 
00488	with that, you will know that your recovery work had reached a deep level.
