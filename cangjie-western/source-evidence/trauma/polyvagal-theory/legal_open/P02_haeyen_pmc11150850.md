1  Polyvagal theory (PVT) was proposed by Porges (1995), who pointed out that approximately 80% of nerve signals are sent from the body to the brain, and only 20% from the brain to the body.
2  This means that bodily signals—sensations, emotions, and physiological changes—provide much of the information the brain needs to function.
3  The phylogenetically ordered response hierarchy regulates autonomic state adaptation to safe, dangerous, and life-threatening environments.
4  There are three levels of stress response, also called the "stress ladder" (Porges 1995, 2021; Dana 2020):
5  Safety and social connection (ventral vagal state): the heart rate is calm and regular; attunement and safe connection to others.
6  Mobilization (sympathetic state): the heart rate accelerates and the muscles tighten in preparation for "fight or flight"; activation: fight-flight response.
7  Immobilization (dorsal vagal state): the heart rate slows; immobilization and dissociation/detachment; the oldest branch.
8  Specific physiological tenets of PVT are subject to dispute (Steffen et al. 2022; Grossman 2023; Verkuil 2023), but the experiences of clients and therapists in clinical practice suggest that it may provide tools that support personal recovery.
9  PVT approaches brain and body not as "either/or" but as interconnected systems.
10 Techniques for "noticing and naming" and "learning to change" are described as the clinical core of PVT-informed work.
