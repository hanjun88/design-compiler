# Evidence: TherapistAssist — "How to use the polyvagal ladder in therapy" (Deb Dana operationalization)
# URL: https://therapistassist.app/learn/how-to/how-to-use-the-polyvagal-ladder
# License: Clinical how-to summary, open page
# Lines numbered.

1  Deb Dana's polyvagal ladder translates Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory into something clients can actually use: three rungs (ventral vagal = safe and social, sympathetic = mobilized fight/flight, dorsal vagal = collapse/freeze) and the question "which rung am I on right now?"
2  The ladder gives a non-pathologizing language for states clients previously had no words for.
3  Step 1 Draw the ladder: top = ventral (safe, connected, can think and feel). Middle = sympathetic (mobilized, anxious, angry, scanning). Bottom = dorsal (numb, collapsed, gone).
4  Step 2 Identify the client's home base: Where do they spend most time? Many trauma clients live in sympathetic-with-dorsal-collapses; high-functioning ACEs survivors often live high-sympathetic and don't know it's not the top.
5  Step 3 Map this week's transitions: Pick 2-3 recent moments. For each: which rung was I on? what bumped me to a new rung? what brought me back? The transitions are the data.
6  Step 4 Build a per-rung regulation menu: Dorsal -> orienting, movement, cold water, naming objects (climbs to sympathetic, which is the path up). Sympathetic -> paced exhale, grounding, co-regulation with a safe person (climbs to ventral). Ventral -> notice it, savor it, mark what helped get there.
7  Step 5 Practice glimmers: Dana's term for small moments of ventral. Assign a daily "glimmer log" — 30 seconds of noticing one small moment of safety per day.
8  Step 6 Use the ladder as a session check-in: "where are you on the ladder right now?" Builds interoception and gives live data during processing work.
9  The path up from dorsal always runs through sympathetic — never skip the middle rung.
10 The intervention sequence for a dorsal moment is always: first mobilize (sympathetic — even a small amount of activation), then regulate the mobilization (paced exhale, grounding, co-regulation), then arrive at ventral.
11 Therapists who try to talk a collapsed client into safety, calm, or connection are skipping the middle rung and reliably fail.
12 "Glimmers" name the small moments of ventral access many trauma clients miss entirely — a dog's tail wag noticed in the corner of vision, the temperature of coffee on the lips, a stranger's smile.
13 Treating ventral as the only "good" state is a pitfall — all three states are adaptive in context.
14 Polyvagal theory has supportive but contested neurophysiology; the clinical framework has wide adoption in trauma-informed care because of its experiential utility, not because of RCT-grade outcome data.
